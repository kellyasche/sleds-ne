Research Questions: Workforce Trajectories of Non-College Graduates in Northeast Minnesota

This phase of the analysis examines workforce participation trajectories among high school graduates in Northeast Minnesota who did not complete a postsecondary credential. Rather than relying on a single point-in-time workforce outcome, the analysis focuses on patterns of labor market attachment during the first five years after exiting formal education, with particular attention to persistence, instability, and regional attachment.

The research is guided by the following questions:

  1. Among non-college graduates, how persistent is being disconnected from sustained workforce participation either in the region or state?

  2. For non-college graduates who experience non-sustained workforce participation, where does employment occur when it is present?

  3. Among individuals categorized as having no Minnesota employment record during the first five years after high school, how persistent is this status?

  4. How are individual characteristics associated with workforce disconnection and subsequent employment trajectories?


Study Population and Group Definitions

Study Population

The analytic population consists of individuals who:

  • Graduated from a high school in Northeast Minnesota, and
  • Did not complete a postsecondary credential (ps.grad = No), and,
  • Have workforce participation records available for at least the first five years after high school graduation OR the first five years after exiting formal post-secondary education.


Definition of Workforce Disconnection

Workforce participation is measured annually during the first five years after high school or existing post-secondary attendance. Individuals are classified as disconnected in a given year if they are categorized as either:

  • Non-sustained workforce participation, or
  • No Minnesota employment record.

Using this annual classification, individuals are grouped based on the persistence of disconnection during years 1 through 5:

  • Persistently Disconnected:
    Disconnected in four or five of the first five years after graduation.

  • Disconnected (Broad Definition):
    Disconnected in three or more of the first five years after graduation.

  • Intermittently Disconnected:
    Disconnected in one or two of the first five years after graduation.

  • **Consistently attached:** Have sustained workforce participation in all five years.

These definitions emphasize sustained labor market detachment rather than short-term transitions.


Regional Attachment

For years in which individuals are employed but not meaningfully attached to the workforce, employment location is classified as:

  • Within Northeast Minnesota, or
  • Outside Northeast Minnesota (elsewhere in Minnesota).

This distinction is used to assess whether workforce disconnection reflects regional underutilization or disengagement from the regional labor market.


Framing Consideration

Together, these definitions allow the analysis to move beyond point-in-time workforce outcomes and toward a trajectory-based understanding of early-career labor market experiences. This approach supports clearer interpretation of workforce instability, recovery, and regional retention among non-college graduates in Northeast Minnesota.


Methodology and Analytic Steps

This analysis examines workforce participation trajectories among non-college graduates in Northeast Minnesota, with a focus on identifying patterns of workforce disconnection, regional attachment, and employment stability during the first five years after high school.

Step 1: Data Preparation and Analytic Population

1.1 Analytic Population

The analytic population is restricted to individuals who:

  • Graduated from a high school in Northeast Minnesota, and
  • Did not complete a postsecondary credential (ps.grad = No), and
  • Have complete workforce participation categorizations for years 1 through 5 after high school or,
  • first 5 years after exiting attendance in a post-secondary institution.

1.2 Annual Workforce Participation Classification

For each individual, annual workforce participation status is identified for: - grad.year.1 - grad.year.2 - grad.year.3 - grad.year.4 - grad.year.5

Each year is classified into one of the following categories:

  • Meaningful workforce participation
  • Not meaningful workforce participation
  • No Minnesota employment record.

It should be noted that “grad.year.x” here means years after exiting formal education, not years after graduating high school.

1.3 Workforce Disconnection Indicators

For each annual observation, a binary disconnection indicator is created:

  • Disconnected (year-level) = Non-sustained WF - NE or MN, or No Minnesota emp record

Using these annual indicators, individuals are grouped based on persistence of disconnection across years 1–5:

  • Persistently Disconnected: Disconnected in 4 or 5 of the first 5 years
  • Disconnected (Broad): Disconnected in 3 of 5 years
  • Intermittently Disconnected: Disconnected in 1–2 of the first 5 years
  • Consistently Attached: Disconnected in 0 of the first 5 years

These categories emphasize sustained labor market attachment or detachment rather than point-in-time outcomes.

1.4 Regional Location of Not Meaningful Employment

For years in which individuals are categorized as Non-sustained WF, employment location is further classified as:

  • Within Northeast Minnesota
  • Outside Northeast Minnesota (elsewhere in Minnesota)
  • Both (simultaneous employment inside and outside the region)

This classification allows identification of regional underemployment versus regional disengagement.

1.5 Employment Intensity and Job Holding

For each individual and each year (years 1–5), the following employment intensity measures are constructed:

  • Number of distinct employers during the year
  • Indicator for multiple job holding (two or more employers)

These measures are used to distinguish underemployment from precarious or fragmented employment.


Step 2: Descriptive and Summary Statistics

2.1 Distribution of Workforce Disconnection

Summary statistics are produced to describe:

  • The proportion of non-college graduates in each workforce disconnection category
  • Differences in persistence and intermittency of disconnection

This establishes the overall scope and severity of workforce disconnection in early adulthood.

2.2 Regional Attachment and Job Structure

Among individuals experiencing workforce disconnection, summary statistics examine:

  • The location of not meaningful employment (within region, outside region, both)
  • Average and median number of employers per year
  • Prevalence of multiple job holding

These summaries clarify whether disconnection reflects local underemployment, out-of-region work, or fragmented employment patterns.


Step 3: Relationship Between Individual Characteristics and Disconnection Trajectories

Step 3 will focus on answering the question of which characteristics are associated with the disconnection persistence categories. This will be a bi-variate analysis.

Independent Variables

We will use the following variables that were selected based on our earlier multiple correspondence analysis.

  • Demographics
    • Gender
    • RaceEthnicity

    • SpecialEdStatus
    • english.learner
  • High school characteristics
    • Dem_Desc

  • High school academics

    • MCA.M
    • MCA.R
    • MCA.S
    • cte.achievement
    • total.cte.courses.taken
  • Post-secondary
    • attended.ps

3.2 Analytic Approach

  • Categorical variables are examined using cross-tabulations and measures of association (e.g., Cramer’s V).
  • Continuous variables are examined using ANOVA.
  • Interpretation emphasizes substantive differences and patterns rather than statistical significance alone.

3.3 Stratified and Comparative Analyses

Where sample sizes permit, additional analyses compare:

  • Persistently disconnected versus intermittently disconnected individuals
  • Individuals disconnected within the region versus outside the region
  • Individuals who transition into meaningful workforce participation versus those who do not

These comparisons help identify which characteristics are associated with stability, recovery, and regional retention.


Framing and Interpretation

This methodology prioritizes a trajectory-based understanding of workforce participation, recognizing that early-career labor market experiences are dynamic rather than static. By combining persistence measures, regional employment location, and job-holding intensity, the analysis aims to provide actionable insights for workforce development and labor force planning in Northeast Minnesota.

Step 3: Workforce Disconnection Indicators

Next we add another category to determine the level of disconnection, if any, for each individual. Disconnected is defined as any individual that was categorized as not having meaningful workforce participation or no Minnesota employment record in a given year one through five years after exiting formal education.

The level of disconnection is defined as the following;

  • Persistently Disconnected: Disconnected in 4 or 5 of the first 5 years
  • Broadly Disconnected: Disconnected in 3 of the first 5 years
  • Intermittently Disconnected: Disconnected in 1–2 of the first 5 years
  • Consistently Attached: Disconnected in 0 of the first 5 years
disconnected.level <- master.annual.wf.five.year.states %>%
  mutate(disconnected = ifelse(states %in% c("Non-sustained WF", "No MN emp record"), "Disconnected", "Attached"),
         disconnected = as.factor(disconnected)) %>%
  group_by(PersonID, disconnected) %>%
  summarize(n = n()) %>%
  ungroup() %>%
  complete(PersonID, disconnected, fill = list(n = 0)) %>%
  filter(disconnected == "Disconnected") %>%
  mutate(disconnected.cat = ifelse(n == 0, "Consistently attached",
                                   ifelse(n %in% c(1,2), "Intermittently disconnected",
                                          ifelse(n == 3, "Broadly disconnected",
                                                 ifelse(n %in% c(4,5), "Persistently disconnected", "A")))),
         disconnected.cat = fct_relevel(disconnected.cat, "Consistently attached", "Intermittently disconnected", "Broadly disconnected", "Persistently disconnected"))

Descriptive analysis

Distribution of Workforce Disconnection

More than half of non-college graduates in Northeast Minnesota are persistently disconnected from sustained workforce participation during their first five years out of school or postsecondary attendance. This is the single largest category by a wide margin — more than double the next-largest group, and more than four times the size of the “Consistently attached” group.

Sustained, uninterrupted workforce attachment is rare. Only about 1 in 8 non-college graduates (11.9%) achieve sustained participation in every one of their first five years. This is essentially tied with the smallest disconnection category (Broadly disconnected, also 11.9%) — meaning a graduate in this population is about as likely to be attached in every year as they are to be disconnected in exactly three of five.

Combining the two most severe categories, nearly two-thirds (65.1%) of non-college graduates spend at least three of their first five years disconnected from sustained workforce participation. Only about a third (34.9%) spend three or more years attached (Intermittent + Consistently attached, loosely).

Overall Interpretation

This distribution reframes the scale of the workforce disconnection problem for non-college graduates in a way that point-in-time snapshots (like the earlier grad.year.1/5/10 distributions) could not fully capture. Rather than disconnection being a transitional, early-career phenomenon that most people eventually move past, the data shows it is the dominant, persistent experience for a majority of this population — over half spend nearly their entire first five years disconnected. This is a markedly starker picture than the single-year distributions examined earlier in this project suggested, and it substantially raises the stakes for Step 3’s question of which characteristics predict landing in the “Persistently disconnected” group specifically, since that group represents the majority experience for non-completers in this region, not an outlier one.

Regional Attachment and Job Structure

Among individuals experiencing workforce disconnection, summary statistics examine: - The location of not meaningful employment (within region, outside region, both) - Average and median number of employers per year - Prevalence of multiple job holding

These summaries clarify whether disconnection reflects local underemployment, out-of-region work, or fragmented employment patterns.

Let’s start with the distribution of the various workforce states with the newly defined categories within “Not meaningful” but not all of them because it will get too messy. Let’s first do the following categories;

  1. Attending ps
  2. Meaningful emp region
  3. Meaningful emp MN
  4. Not meaningful
    1. In only
    2. Out only
    3. Unclassified only
    4. Mixed
  5. No MN emp record

The majority of non-sustained employment in this population has at least some foothold in the regional labor market. Separating “mixed” employment by whether it includes any in-region component shows that 81.1% of all non-sustained employment in Year 1 involves at least partial regional attachment, declining gradually to 76.6% (Year 2), 74.6% (Year 3), 72.8% (Year 4), and 73.5% (Year 5). Only a small and stable share of non-sustained work — roughly 7-8% of the total population, or under 20% of non-sustained work specifically — happens entirely outside the region with zero regional component, and this stays essentially flat across all five years.

This points to regional underutilization, not regional disengagement, as the dominant form of instability in this population. The people captured in non-sustained employment are overwhelmingly not people who’ve left for opportunity elsewhere — they’re present in the regional labor market, working, just not steadily or sufficiently to clear the “meaningful employment” threshold.

The decline in this category over the five years reflects a shrinking pool, not people leaving the region for good. As overall non-sustained employment shrinks (43.4% → 26.4% of the population from Year 1 to Year 5), the in-region-attached share shrinks proportionally, while remaining the dominant form of non-sustained work throughout. Meanwhile, “no MN employment record” keeps growing every year — the real destination for people moving out of non-sustained status increasingly appears to be dropping out of the tracked employment system altogether, not shifting into out-of-region-only work, which stays flat around 7-8% the entire time.

Overall Interpretation

Underutilization — partial, unstable, or insufficient regional employment — is the dominant experience among non-college graduates who aren’t sustainably employed, at every point across the first five years, even as its absolute size shrinks. Combined with the fact that “no MN employment record” is the fastest-growing and eventually largest category overall, the fuller story is: a large share of this population remains meaningfully present in the regional labor force even when their employment doesn’t count as “sustained,” and the real risk that grows over time isn’t people leaving the region for work elsewhere (which stays flat) — it’s people falling out of Minnesota’s tracked employment system entirely. This is an important distinction for workforce and economic developers: the underutilization piece points toward job quality, hours, and employer-engagement questions for people who are already here and already working, while the growing “no MN record” piece points toward a separate and less well-understood question about disengagement, migration, or informal work that this data alone can’t resolve.

Summary: What the Disconnection Analysis Tells Us

  1. Disconnection is not a marginal or transitional experience for this population — it’s the majority experience.** 53.2% of non-college graduates are persistently disconnected (4-5 of their first 5 years), compared to just 11.9% who are consistently attached. Nearly two-thirds (65.1%) spend at least three of five years disconnected. Disconnection among non-college graduates is highly persistent, not a brief early-career phase that most people age out of.
  2. Underutilization — not disengagement — is the dominant form of instability while it lasts.** Among the share of this population not sustainably employed in a given year, the large majority remain at least partially attached to the regional labor market. In Year 1, 81.1% of all non-sustained employment involves some in-region component; this declines gradually but stays above 73% through Year 5. Only a small, flat share (7-8% of the population) works entirely outside the region with no regional attachment at all. These are people who are present and working in the region — just not steadily or sufficiently to clear the “meaningful employment” bar. This is a job-quality and underemployment story, not an outmigration story.
  3. Two genuinely different trends are unfolding simultaneously, and they point in opposite directions.** Sustained employment (regional and elsewhere in Minnesota) grows steadily every single year, from 30.2% of the population in Year 1 to 40.1% by Year 5 — real, consistent progress. At the same time, “no Minnesota employment record” also grows every year, from 26.4% to 33.4%, and grows faster than sustained employment does — overtaking every other single category by Year 5. As non-sustained/underutilized employment shrinks over time, it is not primarily converting into out-of-region work (which stays flat around 7-8%) — it’s converting more into complete disappearance from the tracked system than into any other outcome.
  4. Together, these findings sharpen what “improvement over time” actually means for this population.** It is not a simple story of instability resolving into stability. It’s a population splitting into two increasingly distinct groups: one that’s finding steadier footing (increasingly regional, but also increasingly happy to find that stability elsewhere in the state), and another that is increasingly vanishing from Minnesota’s employment records altogether — a group whose size grows every year and whose underlying cause (true labor force exit, out-of-state migration, or informal/untracked work) can’t be determined from this data alone.

What this sets up for the rest of the analysis: the data has now established that disconnection is persistent, that most non-sustained employment remains regionally rooted rather than reflecting flight to other labor markets, and that the fastest-growing risk is disappearance from the tracked system rather than instability or out-migration. The open pieces are:

  1. directly testing the persistence of “no MN employment record” as its own trajectory (the project’s third research question), and
  2. the planned bivariate work on which characteristics predict landing in “Persistently disconnected” versus “Consistently attached.”

Given how much of the disconnection story is really two distinct phenomena — regional underutilization and disappearance from the record — it may be worth testing predictors against each of these separately, rather than only against the combined four-category disconnection variable, since a single predictor could plausibly explain one of these patterns well and the other poorly.

The categories are defined as;

  • “Non-sustained driven” = this person’s disconnected years were mostly/entirely unstable employment (non-sustained), not disappearing from the record. Example: someone with 4 years of non-sustained work and 0 years of no-record. They were disconnected in the combined sense, but they were always at least working, just not steadily.
  • “No-record driven” = the opposite — this person’s disconnected years were mostly/entirely no employment record at all, not instability. Example: someone with 0 years non-sustained and 4 years no-record. They weren’t cycling through shaky jobs; they mostly just weren’t showing up in Minnesota’s system at all.
  • “Mixed (neither individually persistent)” = this person doesn’t have 4+ years in either single category, but their two counts still add up to 4 or 5 disconnected years total. Example: 2 years non-sustained + 2 years no-record = 4 disconnected years total (enough to be “persistently disconnected” overall), but only 2 in each — not enough on its own to count as “persistent” in either individual measure. This person alternated between the two problems rather than being stuck in one.

The single largest driver of the “persistently disconnected” majority is no-record status, not instability. 40.1% of everyone classified as persistently disconnected got there primarily by disappearing from Minnesota’s tracked employment system for most of their first five years — not by cycling through unstable jobs. Non-sustained employment alone accounts for a smaller share (29.5%).

Nearly a third of the persistently disconnected group (30.4%) doesn’t fit either single-mechanism story at all. These 2,453 people never accumulated enough years in either category alone to qualify as persistent in that specific measure, yet their combined disconnected years (non-sustained + no-record) still reached 4 or 5 out of 5. This is the group entirely invisible in the original combined disconnected.cat measure and in either individual persistence measure on its own — people who bounce between unstable employment and complete absence from the record, rather than settling into one pattern.

Overall Interpretation

Breaking down the “persistently disconnected” majority (53.2% of the full population) shows it is not one uniform experience, but effectively three: two out of five persistently disconnected people (40.1%) are there primarily because they’ve disappeared from Minnesota’s employment system, not because their employment was unstable; three out of ten (29.5%) are there because of genuinely unstable — but still present and trackable — employment; and the remaining three out of ten (30.4%) are a distinct, chronically-alternating group that neither of the two individual persistence measures alone would have identified. This last group deserves particular attention going into Step 3: since they don’t show up as an extreme case on either separated measure individually, any bivariate analysis run only against non_sustained.cat or no_record.cat separately would miss them entirely, and they may warrant their own dedicated comparison (e.g., “mixed” vs. “single-mechanism” persistently disconnected) rather than being folded into either separated analysis.

High school academics

Effect size ranking (Cramér’s V / eta-squared): Took ACT (V=0.075) ≈ CTE achievement (V=0.069) > AP exam (V=0.051) > PSEO (V=0.034, caveat above) for the categorical variables; ACT composite score (eta²≈1.9%) is by far the strongest continuous predictor, followed by total CTE courses (eta²≈0.9%), with MCA Math (0.15%), MCA Reading (0.08%), and MCA Science (0.08%, only barely significant at p=.033) all showing negligible practical effect despite technical statistical significance.

PSEO participation shows a small but measurable protective association. Persistently disconnected: 50.5% of PSEO participants vs. 53.9% of non-participants — a modest 3.4-point gap in the expected direction, consistent with PSEO’s broadly protective (if usually weak) role throughout this project. This is the weakest effect of any high-school-accomplishment variable examined here (V = 0.034), and, as noted above, its sample reflects listwise deletion of missing values rather than an explicit cohort restriction — though given that gap is small (~224 records, 2.2% of the sample), the result itself is unlikely to change materially if re-run on a fully restricted sample.

CTE engagement shows the same clean, monotonic, protective gradient found throughout this entire project. Persistently disconnected rates: CTE Concentrator/Completer (47.5%) < CTE Participant (51.8%) < No CTE (58.0%) — a 10.5-point spread, the cleanest dose-response relationship in this table. Total CTE courses (continuous) confirms this: Persistently disconnected students have significantly fewer CTE courses than every other group, and the “Consistently attached” and “Intermittently disconnected” groups are statistically tied at the high end.

Took ACT (participation) shows the same protective effect found in the earlier point-in-time analysis of this same population. Persistently disconnected: 48.6% of ACT test-takers vs. 56.1% of non-test-takers — a 7.5-point gap favoring test-takers, consistent with ACT participation functioning as a broad marker of engagement/stability for non-completers, not just a college-prep signal that fails to pay off.

ACT score (among test-takers) and AP exam participation both point the opposite direction — the same crossover pattern established earlier in this project. Among test-takers, ACT score is significantly higher for the Persistently disconnected group than every other group (+1.26 vs. Consistently attached, +1.45 vs. Intermittently disconnected, both p < .001) — the Consistently attached and Intermittently disconnected groups are statistically tied at the low end of ACT scores. AP exam shows the same direction categorically: Persistently disconnected is 62.6% among AP-takers vs. 52.7% among non-takers — AP-takers are more likely to be persistently disconnected, not less.

MCA scores show weak, largely one-sided effects, and MCA Science barely clears significance at all. Only the Persistently disconnected vs. Consistently attached contrast reaches significance for MCA Math (−0.123, p < .001) and MCA Reading (−0.080, p = .012) — Persistently disconnected students score modestly lower, but no other pairwise contrast is significant for either subject, and the overall effect sizes are negligible (under 0.2% variance explained for both). MCA Science’s overall F-test is barely significant (p = .033) and only one contrast (Intermittently disconnected vs. Consistently attached) reaches significance — this is the weakest and least reliable of the three MCA subjects here, consistent with its established missingness problems elsewhere in this project.

Overall Interpretation

This set of results replicates, almost variable-for-variable, the same “dual pathways” pattern found throughout this entire project: CTE engagement and simple ACT participation both function as broad protective factors against workforce disconnection, while ACT score and AP exam participation — markers of stronger academic achievement and college-prep orientation — actually predict worse disconnection outcomes for this specifically non-completing population. PSEO participation fits the protective side of this story as well, though as the weakest effect of the group (V = 0.034) — a modest reminder that not every college-preparatory activity carries the same “pulls away from stability” signal as ACT score or AP exam; PSEO, like CTE and ACT participation, appears to function more as a marker of general engagement than of academic ambition specifically. This is a coherent and now well-established story: for students who don’t complete college, technical/vocational engagement and basic test-taking or coursework behavior anchor them to stable workforce participation, while stronger academic credentials without a completed degree appear to correlate with more persistent disconnection — plausibly reflecting a mismatch between academic readiness and the outcome actually achieved. MCA scores add comparatively little independent signal here, with Math and Reading showing only a modest gap concentrated at the extremes (Persistently disconnected vs. Consistently attached) and Science barely registering at all.

Post-secondary

Attending postsecondary at all — regardless of never completing it — shows by far the strongest relationship of any variable examined against this outcome (χ² = 577.90, df = 3, p < .001, Cramér’s V = 0.195), more than double the next-strongest variable (Special Ed status, V = 0.120) and nearly triple ACT participation and CTE achievement.

The gap is large and moves the same population from majority-persistent to roughly a coin flip. Persistently disconnected: 60.2% among those who never attempted postsecondary vs. 41.7% among those who did — an 18.5-point gap. Put differently: someone who never tried college is about 1.4x as likely to be persistently disconnected as someone who attended but didn’t finish.

The pattern holds cleanly across every category, not just at the extremes. Consistently attached is roughly twice as common among those who attended (17.4%) as those who didn’t (8.6%), and Intermittently disconnected is also meaningfully more common among attendees (28.4% vs. 19.7%) — while Broadly disconnected is nearly identical between the two groups (12.5% vs. 11.5%). The entire gap is concentrated in the “Consistently attached” and “Persistently disconnected” tails, with the middle categories barely differing — suggesting attempting postsecondary mostly determines whether someone avoids the most severe end of disconnection, more than it shifts people gradually along the whole spectrum.

Overall Interpretation

This confirms, in the persistence framing, exactly what the earlier point-in-time bivariate analysis found for this same population: simply attempting postsecondary education — even without completing it — is the single strongest predictor of workforce outcomes examined anywhere in this project, stronger than every demographic characteristic, high school context variable, and academic achievement measure combined. This reinforces that “non-completer” is not one uniform population; whether someone ever tried college at all appears to function as the dividing line separating a group that’s largely protected from persistent disconnection from one that overwhelmingly is not. Given this is by a wide margin the strongest bivariate relationship found in this entire disconnection analysis, attended.ps should be treated as a central variable in any eventual multivariate confirmation of these findings, not just one item among many.

The Story This Data Tells

One variable dominates everything else: simply attempting postsecondary education, whether or not it was ever completed, is by far the strongest predictor of long-term disconnection persistence found anywhere in this analysis. At nearly double the next-strongest effect, attended.ps isn’t just the top variable — it reframes the whole population. “Non-completer” is not one group; someone who tried college and stopped out looks statistically much more like a “successful” workforce participant than someone who never tried at all.

Two demographic characteristics — Special Education status and English learner status — stand out as the strongest individual-level barriers, each showing 13-20 point gaps in persistent disconnection. Both replicate patterns established throughout this entire project. Race/Ethnicity shows a more modest summary statistic but conceals large, real gaps for individual minority groups (Hispanic, Black, and Asian/PI graduates all show persistent-disconnection rates roughly 19-22 points above White graduates) — the modest V reflects White students’ large sample share, not a weak underlying relationship.

A clean “dual pathways” pattern runs through every high-school-accomplishment variable, replicating what this project has found again and again: CTE engagement and simple ACT participation both protect against persistent disconnection, while ACT score and AP exam participation — markers of stronger academic achievement rather than just engagement — actually predict worse outcomes for this specifically non-completing population. This is now a thoroughly confirmed pattern across every outcome examined in this entire project: for non-completers, technical/vocational engagement and basic test-taking behavior anchor people to stability, while stronger academic credentials without a finished degree correlate with more persistent disconnection, plausibly reflecting a mismatch between preparation and outcome. PSEO participation fits this same protective side of the story, though weakly.

High school geographic and economic context — one of the most consistently important variable groups everywhere else in this project — shows essentially no relationship with overall disconnection persistence. Average local wages and unemployment rate are both flatly non-significant, and district rural/urban classification, while technically significant, is the weakest effect found anywhere in this analysis (V = 0.025) and driven purely by the large sample size rather than any real substantive gap. This is a genuine departure from earlier findings in this project and raises a real question: does local economic context matter for some forms of disconnection but not others, in a way the combined measure is hiding?

What This Sets Up

The near-total absence of a local-context effect here is the most important open question heading into the separated analyses. Since local wages and unemployment showed real relationships with where people worked in the earlier point-in-time analysis, but show nothing here against overall persistence, one clear hypothesis to test: local economic conditions may specifically predict no-record persistence (people leaving a weak local labor market entirely) but have little bearing on non-sustained persistence (people who stay and struggle regardless of local conditions) — or vice versa. This is exactly the kind of divergence the non_sustained.cat / no_record.cat comparison was built to catch, and it’s the first thing worth checking once those two tables are in hand.

Demographic variables

The ranking of demographic variables shifts dramatically compared to the combined disconnected.cat measure — this is exactly the divergence the separation was designed to catch. Against non_sustained.cat: Economic status (V=0.083) > Gender (V=0.079) > Special Ed status (V=0.045) > Race/Ethnicity (V=0.041) > English learner (V=0.033). Compare this to the disconnected.cat ranking, where Special Ed (0.120) and English learner (0.105) were by far the two strongest predictors, and Economic status (0.061) and Gender (0.047) were comparatively modest. The two strongest demographic predictors of overall disconnection are now the two weakest predictors of instability specifically — a complete reordering, not just a rescaling.

This strongly suggests Special Ed and English learner status drive overall disconnection primarily through no-record status, not through unstable employment. Special Ed’s gap here is real but modest (Persistently non-sustained: 18.2% vs. 14.9%, a 3.3-point gap) — a fraction of the 13.3-point gap seen in the combined measure. English learner’s gap is smaller still (17.9% vs. 15.5%), and its “Never non-sustained” rate is actually higher for English learners than non-English-learners (33.5% vs. 29.6%) — meaning English learners are somewhat less likely to experience unstable-but-present employment, the opposite direction from what the combined measure’s large gap implied. Both findings point the same way: whatever is driving these two groups’ strong overall disconnection numbers, it isn’t instability.

Economic status and Gender, by contrast, show their strongest relationships specifically here — concentrated in the instability mechanism. Economic disadvantage: Persistently non-sustained 16.9% vs. 14.4%, and more strikingly, Never non-sustained 26.3% vs. 33.5% — a real, substantial gap in who avoids instability altogether. Gender shows a similar pattern: Persistently non-sustained 17.9% (female) vs. 14.1% (male), and Never non-sustained 27.2% vs. 31.8% — women are both more likely to experience persistent instability and less likely to avoid it altogether. Notably, gender’s effect size here (V=0.079) is nearly double its effect size against the combined measure (V=0.047) — instability is where gender’s relationship to workforce outcomes actually lives, and it was being diluted in the combined measure.

Race/Ethnicity reveals the clearest individual-group divergence of the whole set: Asian/PI graduates. In the combined measure, Asian/PI showed the second-highest persistent-disconnection rate of any group (69.8%). Here, against non-sustained specifically, Asian/PI shows the lowest persistently-non-sustained rate of any racial group (12.1%, below even White at 15.2%) and by far the highest “Never non-sustained” rate (40.5%, roughly 10 points above every other group). Black graduates show the opposite pattern — the highest persistently-non-sustained rate (22.3%) of any group, a genuine instability-specific finding not fully visible in the combined measure.

Overall Interpretation

This is the clearest confirmation yet that disconnected.cat was masking meaningfully different underlying stories for different groups. Special Ed status and English learner status — the two strongest predictors of overall disconnection — turn out to have little to do with employment instability specifically; their disconnection is concentrated in no-record status (to be confirmed once we run these same variables against no_record.cat). Economic status and Gender, conversely, show their real signal here, in instability specifically, and were understated by the combined measure. Race/Ethnicity shows the most striking individual reversal: Asian/PI graduates are the least likely of any group to experience unstable employment, despite being among the most likely to be persistently disconnected overall — meaning their disconnection story is almost certainly a no-record story, not an instability story, while Black graduates show the opposite: a genuine instability-driven pattern that the combined measure partially obscured.

All three high-school-characteristic variables show essentially no relationship with non-sustained employment persistence — an even more complete null than what these variables showed against the combined disconnected.cat measure.

High school academics

A note on scale carried over from before: pseo.participant’s n = 10,352 reflects the same near-complete, mostly-benign PSEO tracking gap discussed earlier — not a new concern.

Several variables show dramatically weaker effects here than against disconnected.cat — and in a few cases, the direction actually reverses. This is the richest set of divergences found yet in this comparison.

ACT score’s crossover effect nearly disappears. Against the combined measure, ACT score showed a strong, clean effect (eta² ≈ 1.9%, Persistently disconnected significantly higher than every other group). Here, the overall effect shrinks by nearly 90% (eta² ≈ 0.21%), and only one contrast survives (Persistently vs. Intermittently non-sustained, +0.621, p = .003) — the clean, complete ordering found against the combined measure is gone. This strongly confirms the hypothesis raised earlier: ACT score’s relationship with overall disconnection is mostly a no-record story, not an instability story — something to verify directly once we reach no_record.cat.

Took-ACT and PSEO participation both flip from protective to flat-or-reversed. Against disconnected.cat, both showed real protective effects (took.ACT: 7.5-point gap favoring test-takers; PSEO: 3.4-point gap favoring participants). Here: took.ACT shows almost no gap in Persistently non-sustained (16.0% vs. 15.5%) and, more tellingly, test-takers show a lower “Never non-sustained” rate (27.4% vs. 31.5%) — the opposite of protective. PSEO shows a similar reversal: Persistently non-sustained is actually higher for participants (17.2% vs. 15.6%). Both of these variables’ protective effects against overall disconnection appear to run almost entirely through avoiding no-record status, not through avoiding instability — a second and third confirmation of the same pattern found for ACT score.

CTE shows a genuinely more complex, non-monotonic story here than the clean gradient found against the combined measure. cte.achievement’s effect shrinks (V = 0.039 vs. 0.069) and its shape changes: at the “Persistently non-sustained” end, the expected protective gradient holds (CTE Concentrator/Completer 13.9% < Participant 15.8% < No CTE 16.8%), but at “Intermittently non-sustained,” the direction reverses — CTE Concentrators/Completers show the highest intermittent rate (43.2%), not the lowest. total.cte.courses.taken confirms this isn’t noise: Intermittently non-sustained shows significantly more CTE courses than every other category, including Never non-sustained. This suggests CTE engagement protects specifically against severe/persistent instability, while plausibly contributing to brief, intermittent instability — possibly reflecting the short-term employment transitions inherent to CTE work-based-learning placements (internships, apprenticeship-style courses ending and restarting).

AP exam is the one variable that holds up nearly unchanged. Persistently non-sustained: 20.7% (AP) vs. 15.4% (non-AP), V = 0.042 — comparable in both direction and magnitude to its effect against the combined measure (V = 0.051). AP exam appears to be a genuine instability-specific predictor, not one whose effect runs through no-record status.

MCA scores show a genuine subject-specific divergence: Science is now significant, Math and Reading are not. This is the reverse of the pattern found against disconnected.cat, where Math and Reading (weakly) reached significance and Science barely did. Here, MCA Science shows a clean, real effect (eta² ≈ 0.19%, p < .001): “Never non-sustained” scores significantly higher than all three other categories, which aren’t distinguished from each other — any instability at all associates with lower Science scores, not a dose-response pattern. Math (p = .069) and Reading (p = .230) are both non-significant here.

Overall Interpretation

This is the most revealing comparison yet between the combined and separated measures. This is the single largest divergence found in this entire comparison, and it confirms the emerging pattern across nearly every other variable in this table. Against disconnected.cat, attended.ps was by far the dominant predictor (V = 0.195, nearly triple the next-strongest variable). Here, its effect drops to V = 0.077 — less than 40% of its original size, and no longer anywhere near the strongest predictor in the table (now comparable to Economic status and Gender, both around 0.08).

The most telling detail: “Never non-sustained” shows almost no gap at all between attendees and non-attendees (30.2% vs. 29.7%). Compare this to the combined measure, where “Consistently attached” showed attendees at roughly double the rate of non-attendees (17.4% vs. 8.6%). This means attempting postsecondary does essentially nothing to prevent instability from occurring at all — the massive protective effect seen against overall disconnection must be running almost entirely through avoiding no-record status, not through avoiding unstable employment. This is now the fourth variable (after ACT score, ACT participation, and PSEO) to show this same pattern, making it look less like a series of individual coincidences and more like a structural feature of this population: no-record status, not instability, is where most of these “protective” variables’ real effect lives.

Where attendance does matter here is in preventing instability from becoming chronic, not in preventing it altogether — the same reallocation pattern found for CTE. Attendees show a notably higher Intermittently non-sustained rate (43.6% vs. 37.6%) but a notably lower Persistently non-sustained rate (12.8% vs. 17.4%) and a modestly lower Broadly non-sustained rate (13.4% vs. 15.3%). In other words: attendees are just as likely to experience some instability as non-attendees, but when they do, it tends to resolve rather than persist — echoing exactly the pattern found for CTE achievement and total CTE courses.

Overall Interpretation

attended.ps’s enormous effect on overall disconnection turns out to be concentrated almost entirely in the no-record mechanism, not instability — attendees are no more likely to avoid unstable employment altogether than non-attendees, but they are considerably more likely to have that instability resolve rather than become chronic, and (presumably, pending the no_record.cat results) considerably less likely to disappear from the tracked system entirely. This is a genuinely important refinement of what was, until now, the single strongest finding in this whole disconnection analysis: attempting college doesn’t buy someone stability from day one, it appears to buy them resilience against instability turning permanent, and — more importantly — protection against falling out of the system altogether.

The Story This Data Tells

This comparison against disconnected.cat produced the clearest confirmation yet that overall disconnection persistence was hiding two genuinely different mechanisms. Four separate variables — ACT composite score, ACT participation, PSEO participation, and (most dramatically) attempting postsecondary at all — showed dramatically weaker, flat, or even reversed relationships with instability specifically, despite being among the strongest predictors of overall disconnection. This isn’t a coincidence repeated four times; it’s a structural pattern. These variables’ apparent protective power against disconnection overall is running almost entirely through avoiding no-record status, not through avoiding unstable employment — a hypothesis the no_record.cat analysis will now be able to test directly and, most likely, confirm.

The two demographic variables that mattered most for overall disconnection barely matter here, and vice versa. Special Ed status and English learner status — the two strongest predictors of disconnected.cat — collapse to two of the weakest predictors of instability specifically. Economic status and Gender, comparatively modest against the combined measure, become the strongest predictors here, with Gender’s effect size nearly doubling. This is a clean reallocation: whatever drives Special Ed and English learner students toward overall disconnection isn’t instability; whatever drives economically disadvantaged and female graduates toward disconnection largely is.

Race/Ethnicity’s most striking finding is a reversal for Asian/PI graduates specifically. Despite showing one of the highest persistent-disconnection rates overall, Asian/PI graduates show the lowest rate of persistent instability and the highest rate of avoiding instability altogether — meaning their overall disconnection story is almost certainly a no-record story, not an instability one. Black graduates show the opposite: a genuine, instability-specific disadvantage not fully visible in the combined measure.

A second, more subtle pattern emerged specifically for CTE-related variables and postsecondary attendance: they don’t prevent instability from happening, they prevent it from becoming chronic. Both CTE achievement/course count and attended.ps show a genuine reallocation from “persistent” toward “intermittent” instability, rather than a clean reduction in instability overall. This is a real, substantively interesting nuance the combined measure’s clean monotonic story didn’t reveal.

High school geographic and economic context shows no relationship with instability whatsoever — all three variables (Dem_Desc, local wages, local unemployment) are non-significant, continuing the pattern already found against the combined measure and strengthening the case that local context, if it matters at all in this framework, matters for the no-record mechanism specifically.

MCA subjects flip which one matters. Against the combined measure, Math and Reading were (weakly) significant and Science was borderline; here, only Science is significant, and Math/Reading are not. This is a genuine, if modest, divergence worth noting but not over-interpreting given how small all three effects are.

What This Sets Up

Nearly every major finding in this table points toward the same next step: no_record.cat should show the mirror image of what’s missing here. Specifically watch for: ACT score, ACT participation, PSEO, and attended.ps all showing much larger effects against no-record status than they did here; Special Ed and English learner status re-emerging as strong predictors; and local economic/geographic context finally showing a real relationship, if it’s going to show one anywhere. If that pattern holds, it will confirm that disconnected.cat’s “persistent disconnection” majority was really two distinct populations all along — one defined by chronic instability (driven by economic status, gender, AP exam, and to some extent CTE/attendance patterns), and one defined by disappearing from the tracked system entirely (driven by demographic and academic-achievement/college-engagement variables).

High school characteristics

Contrary to the hypothesis raised after the non_sustained.cat results, local economic context still does not show a strong or consistent relationship here — this needs to be stated plainly rather than reframed to fit expectations.

Average local wages remains completely non-significant (F(3,15165) = 0.67, p = .573, eta² ≈ 0.01%) — the third and final null result for this variable across all three dependent variables in this analysis. Local wage levels appear to have no bearing on any form of disconnection examined in this entire project’s non-completer analysis.

Average local unemployment rate does reach significance here, but the pattern is small and doesn’t fit a simple story. F(3,15165) = 6.21, p < .001, though eta² is still tiny (≈0.12%). The Tukey contrasts show something genuinely counterintuitive: Persistently no-record shows a significantly lower average local unemployment rate than both Intermittently no-record (−0.137, p < .001) and Broadly no-record (−0.123, p = .044) — the opposite of what you’d expect if a weak local labor market were pushing people out of the tracked system entirely. Intermittently no-record shows the highest rate, modestly above Never no-record (+0.075, p = .040). This is not a clean dose-response relationship, and given how small the effect size is, I’d be cautious about building a narrative around it rather than treating it as a genuine, well-supported pattern.

Dem_Desc reaches significance but remains one of the weakest effects found anywhere in this analysis (V = 0.021, p = .038, barely below the .05 threshold). The direction is consistent with the combined measure: Persistently no-record rises modestly with urbanicity (18.8% Entirely rural → 21.4% Town/rural mix → 21.5% Urban/town/rural mix), a small gradient in the same direction found against disconnected.cat.

Overall Interpretation

The hypothesis that local economic and geographic context would finally show a real relationship here — given that wages and unemployment mattered substantially for where sustained employment happened in the earlier point-in-time analysis — does not hold up. Local wages remain flatly irrelevant across all three disconnection measures, and while local unemployment rate does reach statistical significance here, the effect is tiny and the direction (persistent no-record status associated with lower, not higher, local unemployment) runs counter to a simple “weak local economy pushes people out of the system” story. This is worth reporting honestly as a negative or inconclusive finding rather than forcing an explanation: high school geographic and economic context, across all three framings of disconnection examined in this analysis, is simply not where the meaningful predictive signal lives for this population. Whatever drives long-term workforce disconnection for non-college graduates in Northeast Minnesota, it is not primarily rooted in the local labor market conditions of the high school they attended.

High school academics

This table delivers the clearest, most complete confirmation yet of the hypotheses built up across this entire comparison — nearly every variable’s “true home” is now visible.

Took ACT (participation) shows its strongest effect of any table in this entire analysis, confirming it is almost entirely a no-record phenomenon. V = 0.098 here — actually larger than its effect against the combined measure (V = 0.075) and dramatically larger than against instability (V = 0.045, where the direction had even reversed). Persistently no-record: 16.8% (test-takers) vs. 24.2% (non-test-takers) — a 7.4-point gap. Never no-record: 56.3% vs. 47.6%. The earlier finding that ACT participation showed a flat-or-reversed relationship with instability, combined with this result, confirms conclusively: ACT participation protects against disappearing from the tracked system, and has essentially nothing to do with avoiding unstable employment.

PSEO participation shows the same pattern, and even more starkly than took.ACT relative to its own combined-measure effect. V = 0.070 here vs. 0.034 against the combined measure and a reversed, adverse direction against instability. Persistently no-record: 16.9% (PSEO) vs. 22.8% (non-PSEO) — a 5.9-point gap, nearly double what appeared in the combined measure. PSEO’s entire protective effect against overall disconnection lives here.

ACT composite score confirms the same story with a distinctive “threshold” shape. eta² ≈ 1.50% here (vs. 1.87% against the combined measure, vs. a mere 0.21% against instability) — nearly all of ACT score’s crossover effect is concentrated in no-record status. The shape is notable: Never no-record shows a significantly lower score than all three other categories (all three contrasts vs. Never are significant, roughly +0.93 to +1.33), but Intermittently, Broadly, and Persistently no-record are not distinguished from each other. This is a threshold effect, not a dose-response — any amount of no-record experience associates with a meaningfully higher ACT score among test-takers, but more years of it doesn’t compound the gap further.

CTE (both achievement and course count) shows a real, substantial effect here, comparable in strength to the combined measure and clearly stronger than its effect on instability. cte.achievement: V = 0.058 (vs. 0.069 combined, 0.039 instability), with Persistently no-record showing a clean gradient — CTE Concentrator/Completer (18.1%) < Participant (19.0%) < No CTE (25.0%), a 6.9-point spread, the widest specific gap in this table. total.cte.courses.taken confirms this: eta² ≈ 0.66%, with a real, largely monotonic protective pattern (Persistently no-record shows significantly fewer courses than every other category). This differs from the instability findings, where CTE showed a non-monotonic, reallocation pattern rather than a clean reduction — CTE’s protective effect is concentrated more in preventing disappearance from the record than in preventing chronic instability.

AP exam shows its weakest effect of any table so far, and a genuinely different shape. V = 0.030 (vs. 0.051 combined, 0.042 instability) — AP exam’s effect is actually more balanced between the two mechanisms than most other variables, tilting slightly toward instability rather than no-record. Notably, Persistently no-record is nearly identical between AP-takers and non-takers (21.3% vs. 21.3%) — AP’s effect here is concentrated entirely in the “Never” and “Intermittently” categories, not at the severe end.

MCA subjects show small, largely consistent effects across all three, similar in size to what was found against the combined measure — all three reach significance (eta² ≈ 0.09-0.11%), with Math and Reading showing the expected direction (lower scores among no-record groups) and Science showing, curiously, the opposite direction for the Persistently no-record contrast specifically (+0.081, p = .005) — a small, likely not very meaningful reversal given Science’s well-established missingness problems in this project, worth noting but not over-interpreting.

This table completes the picture the entire non_sustained.cat/no_record.cat comparison was designed to reveal. ACT participation, PSEO participation, and (from the earlier table) attempting postsecondary at all are now confirmed as variables whose apparent “broad protective” effect against overall disconnection is almost entirely a no-record story — none of them meaningfully reduce instability, but all three substantially reduce the risk of disappearing from Minnesota’s tracked employment system. CTE shows a more genuinely dual-mechanism effect: real in both, but somewhat stronger here. AP exam is the interesting exception — its effect is the most evenly split of any accomplishment variable, and notably weaker here than elsewhere, suggesting it isn’t primarily a no-record-driving factor either, but a somewhat diffuse predictor of both mild instability and mild no-record risk without much force behind either.

Full Synthesis: Workforce Disconnection Among Non-College Graduates in Northeast Minnesota

Executive Summary

This analysis examined the 58% of Northeast Minnesota high school graduates who never completed a postsecondary credential, tracing their workforce participation across the first five years after exiting formal education. Three major findings anchor this work:

  1. Disconnection is not a transitional phase — it is the majority experience. 53.2% of non-college graduates are persistently disconnected from sustained employment (4-5 of their first 5 years), and only 11.9% are consistently attached.

  2. “Disconnection” is not one phenomenon — it is at least two, and they behave completely differently. Separating unstable-but-present employment (non-sustained work) from complete absence from Minnesota’s tracked system (no-record status) revealed that these two mechanisms have almost entirely different predictors, different trajectories over time, and different underlying stories.

  3. Underutilization, not disengagement, is the dominant experience for those who aren’t stably employed — 73-81% of non-sustained employment involves at least partial regional attachment, meaning most instability reflects people working in the region, just not enough to be “sustained,” not people leaving for opportunity elsewhere.


The Story This Data Tells

Part 1: The scale and shape of the problem

Picture the roughly 15,000 non-college graduates who make up this population. In their first year out, they’re widely dispersed — a little over a third in unstable work, roughly a fifth each in no-record status, still-attending-postsecondary, and sustained regional employment. But this snapshot obscures the real story: tracked over five years, this population sorts into a stark and durable pattern. Just over half end up persistently disconnected; barely one in eight ever achieve full, sustained attachment. Two very different trajectories are unfolding simultaneously — sustained employment does grow steadily every year (30.2% → 40.1% of the population from Year 1 to Year 5) — but “no Minnesota employment record” grows even faster, overtaking every other category by Year 5. Non-sustained employment, meanwhile, consistently shrinks — but critically, when we looked closely at where that non-sustained employment happens, we found the large majority (73-81%, holding steady across all five years) involves at least partial regional attachment. This is underutilization, not outmigration: most people who aren’t stably employed are still here, working, just not enough.

Part 2: Two distinct disconnection mechanisms hiding inside one number

The single most important methodological move in this analysis was splitting “disconnected” into its two components — non-sustained employment and no employment record at all — and testing each independently. This wasn’t a formality; it fundamentally changed the picture:

  • The two measures have genuinely different shapes. Non-sustained persistence is front-loaded toward the intermittent middle (39.8% of the population) and shrinks steadily — a phenomenon that resolves over time. No-record persistence is bimodal (51.0% never, 21.3% persistent) and, once someone crosses into the “broad” category, they’re far more likely to escalate into “persistent” than to plateau — a phenomenon that compounds.
  • Decomposing the “persistently disconnected” majority revealed three roughly equal populations, not one. 40.1% of the persistently disconnected got there mainly through no-record status, 29.5% mainly through instability, and 30.4% through a genuine, chronic alternation between both that neither individual measure alone would have identified.
  • Nearly every bivariate predictor split cleanly onto one side of this divide or the other, and in several cases the split was total.

Part 3: Which characteristics drive which mechanism

Demographics split almost perfectly across the two mechanisms. Special Education status and English learner status — the two strongest predictors of overall disconnection — turned out to be driven almost entirely by no-record status; both showed weak, sometimes reversed relationships with instability. Economic status and Gender showed the opposite pattern: comparatively modest against the combined measure, but the strongest demographic predictors of instability specifically, with Gender’s effect size nearly doubling once isolated.

Race/Ethnicity revealed the most consequential and actionable divergence in the whole analysis. Three different racial/ethnic groups face three different forms of disadvantage: American Indian graduates show the highest persistent-instability rate of any group but look no different from White graduates on no-record status; Hispanic and Asian/PI graduates show the opposite — roughly double the persistent no-record rate of White graduates, but Asian/PI graduates actually show the lowest persistent-instability rate of any group; Black graduates show a real, more moderate elevation on both. Treating “disconnection” as one undifferentiated outcome would have completely obscured these three distinct patterns.

High school accomplishments split into three groups of variables, not two. ACT participation, PSEO participation, and ACT composite score all turned out to be almost purely no-record predictors — their apparent broad protective effect against overall disconnection nearly vanished or reversed once instability was isolated. AP exam participation was the reverse case — a real instability predictor with almost no no-record effect. CTE engagement and, most importantly, attempting postsecondary education at all were the two variables that showed genuine, meaningful effects on both mechanisms — attended.ps in particular remained the single strongest predictor in this entire analysis precisely because it’s the only variable that doesn’t have a blind spot on either side of the divide.

High school geographic and economic context was, across all three framings of disconnection, essentially irrelevant. Local wages showed no relationship with any outcome examined. Local unemployment rate reached significance only against no-record status, but the effect was tiny and ran in a counterintuitive direction. Whatever drives long-term disconnection for this population, it is not rooted in the local economic conditions of the high school they attended — a genuine and somewhat surprising departure from what these same variables showed throughout the rest of this project’s college-completer analyses.

Part 4: A unifying picture

Put all of this together, and a clear structural story emerges. This population’s long-term workforce outcomes are shaped by two largely independent forces: whether someone ever engaged with the postsecondary system at all (attendance, ACT, PSEO) — which mainly determines whether they stay visible in Minnesota’s tracked employment system — and a separate set of demographic and programmatic factors (economic status, gender, CTE, AP) that mainly determine whether the employment they do have is stable. Geography and local economic conditions, so influential elsewhere in this project, play almost no role here. And underneath both mechanisms sits a genuine equity story: different demographic groups aren’t just disconnected at different rates, they’re disconnected in different ways — a distinction that matters enormously for what kind of intervention would actually help.


What This Sets Up for the Multivariate Stage

This bivariate work points toward a clear multivariate design, directly analogous to the DV1a/DV1b structure used earlier in this project’s college-completer analysis:

  1. Two separate model sequences, not one combined model — one for non_sustained.cat persistence, one for no_record.cat persistence — since a single combined model would recreate the same masking problem this entire analysis was built to solve.
  2. attended.ps belongs in both models as a foundational predictor, given it’s the only variable shown to matter for both mechanisms.
  3. Expect substantial attenuation for Special Ed and English learner status once academic/accomplishment variables enter the no-record model — mirroring the exact pattern found throughout the rest of this project (e.g., the MCA-missingness masking effect discovered in the college-completer work).
  4. Local geographic/economic variables can likely be deprioritized or dropped from both models given their consistent null results across all three outcome framings — though worth retaining at least once to formally confirm rather than assume.
  5. Race/Ethnicity should be watched closely for interaction effects — given that AI, Hispanic/Asian-PI, and Black graduates show genuinely different mechanisms of disadvantage, a single additive coefficient per race category may not fully capture what’s happening, and this is worth flagging as a candidate for deeper stratified analysis (per the original methodology’s Step 3.3) even if it’s not built into the initial multivariate pass.
