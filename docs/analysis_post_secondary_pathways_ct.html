Dependent Variable

The cross-tab and ANOVA analysis exploring relationships between demographic, high school, and post-secondary variables with employment outcomes highlighted the importance of post-secondary variables in an individuals journey to meaningful employment inside and outside the region. Due to this, it makes sense to explore if some of the other variables play a role in post-secondary outcomes - whether an individual attends, graduates, the type of institution they attend and the location.

We will explore this here. We will explore this in three sections;

Tier 1: Core Postsecondary Access and Attainment

These outcomes are most foundational and closely tied to employment.

1. attended.ps.within.first.year.hsgrad

Question: Who transitions directly to postsecondary? Why it matters: Strongly linked to long-term success and minimizes “disconnection.”

2. attended.ps (ever attended postsecondary)

Question: Who attends postsecondary at all? Why it matters: Broader view than immediate enrollment.

3. ps.grad (graduated from postsecondary)

Question: Who completes a credential? Why it matters: Credential completion is often the real driver of employment outcomes.

🔹 Tier 2: Type of Postsecondary Experience

This outcome differentiate the nature of the experience, which may shape career pathways and economic outcomes.

4. highest.cred.level

Question: Who earns certificates, associate degrees, bachelor’s degrees? Why it matters: Different credentials yield different labor market returns.

Tier 3: Location of Postsecondary Experience

These outcomes reveal mobility, local engagement, or place-based equity patterns.

5. ps.grad.location

Question: Who stays in the region for school? Why it matters: Tied to affordability and regional workforce planning.

Tier 1: Core Postsecondary Access and Attainment

Tier 1 examined how demographic background, high school context, and high school accomplishments predict three key milestones: (1) immediate enrollment (“attended.ps.within.first.year.hsgrad”), (2) ever attending postsecondary (“attended.ps”), and (3) completing a credential (“ps.grad”).

Key Findings

  1. Demographics are the strongest and most consistent predictors across all three outcomes.
    • Economic Status: Economically disadvantaged students lagged behind at every stage—less likely to enroll immediately, less likely to attend at all, and much less likely to graduate.
    • Race/Ethnicity: White and Asian/PI students consistently had higher enrollment and graduation rates, while American Indian, Black, and Hispanic students faced persistent barriers at all stages, with the largest disparities at completion.
    • Special Education Status: Students with special education status showed markedly lower rates of enrollment, attendance, and especially graduation.
  2. High School Characteristics showed weak associations.
    • School Locale: Rural, mixed, and urban settings had minimal differences in postsecondary pathways.
    • Community Economic Conditions: Students from lower-unemployment, higher-wage areas were modestly more likely to enroll, attend, and graduate, but these effects were smaller than individual-level demographics or accomplishments.
  3. High School Accomplishments were powerful predictors, especially for enrollment and graduation.
    • Academic Achievement: ACT and MCA scores (particularly Reading and Science) were strongly linked to both immediate enrollment and graduation, showing that academic readiness is a key driver of persistence and attainment.
    • PSEO Participation: The single strongest programmatic predictor across all outcomes. PSEO students were much more likely to enroll immediately, attend at all, and eventually graduate.
    • CTE Involvement: Higher CTE course-taking and concentrator/completor status were associated with lower likelihood of both enrollment and graduation, suggesting that intensive CTE pathways align more with direct workforce entry than with postsecondary progression.

Overall Conclusion:

Tier 1 analysis reveals that postsecondary access and attainment are shaped by both structural inequities and academic preparation. Demographic barriers—economic disadvantage, special education status, and race/ethnicity—consistently depress outcomes at every stage. Academic readiness (ACT and MCA scores) and program participation (especially PSEO) are the most powerful positive predictors of successful transitions and completion. In contrast, CTE intensity is inversely associated with postsecondary outcomes, reflecting its alignment with alternative workforce pathways.

Together, these findings suggest that strategies to improve postsecondary success must focus on closing equity gaps, bolstering academic readiness, and expanding access to PSEO and similar dual-enrollment programs, while also balancing the role of CTE in supporting both workforce entry and potential academic progression.

Conclusion:
Immediate postsecondary enrollment is shaped most strongly by student characteristics (economic status, special education status, race/ethnicity), program participation (PSEO), and academic achievement (MCA and ACT scores). Community economic conditions (unemployment and wages) also matter but to a lesser extent, while school locale and CTE achievement have minimal influence. This suggests that interventions targeting equity gaps, strengthening academic preparation, and expanding access to PSEO could have the greatest impact on improving direct transitions to postsecondary education.

Overall Conclusion

Tier 3 results show that geographic mobility in postsecondary completion is closely tied to academic strength, program participation, and demographics.

  • Students who are academically stronger or PSEO participants are more likely to graduate away from their home region, either elsewhere in Minnesota or out of state.

In sum, Tier 3 highlights a divide between mobile, academically prepared students who pursue credentials beyond their home region, and students facing structural or programmatic constraints who are more likely to complete locally. Summary of Postsecondary Pathways Analysis (All Tiers)

This study examined how demographics, high school characteristics, and high school accomplishments shape postsecondary pathways across three tiers of outcomes:

  1. Tier 1: Core Postsecondary Access and Attainment
    • Immediate enrollment (attended.ps.within.first.year.hsgrad)
    • Ever attended postsecondary (attended.ps)
    • Postsecondary graduation (ps.grad)
  2. Tier 2: Type of Postsecondary Experience
    • Highest credential earned (highest.cred.level)
  3. Tier 3: Location of Postsecondary Experience
    • Location of postsecondary graduation (ps.grad.location)

Key Findings by Tier

Tier 1: Core Access and Attainment

  • Demographics were the strongest and most consistent predictors. Economic disadvantage, race/ethnicity (especially American Indian, Black, and Hispanic students), and special education status all reduced likelihood of enrollment, attendance, and completion.
  • High School Characteristics (locale, local unemployment, wages) had weak and inconsistent effects.
  • High School Accomplishments were powerful predictors. Higher ACT/MCA scores and PSEO participation strongly boosted immediate enrollment, persistence, and graduation. In contrast, greater CTE engagement was associated with lower enrollment and graduation, aligning with workforce entry rather than postsecondary continuation.

Tier 2: Type of Postsecondary Experience (Highest Credential Earned)

  • Economic disadvantage and special education status created the steepest barriers to earning a bachelor’s or higher degree, with these groups concentrated in “Some college” or sub-associate credentials.
  • Racial disparities persisted: White and Asian/PI students were far more likely to complete bachelor’s or graduate degrees, while American Indian, Black, and Hispanic students were more likely to stop at associate’s or leave with no credential.
  • Academic readiness and PSEO participation were the strongest positive predictors of bachelor’s+ attainment. Students with higher ACT/MCA scores and PSEO experience were much more likely to progress to advanced credentials.
  • CTE engagement again tracked with sub-baccalaureate attainment, with concentrators/completors more likely to finish with associate’s or stop at “Some college.”

Tier 3: Location of Postsecondary Graduation

  • Demographics shaped mobility: disadvantaged, American Indian, Hispanic, and special education students were most likely to graduate within their high school region, while non-disadvantaged, White, and Asian/PI students showed greater mobility (elsewhere in MN or out-of-state).
  • High school characteristics mattered modestly. Rural students tended to leave their region but stay within Minnesota, while students from stronger labor markets (lower unemployment, higher wages) were more likely to graduate in-region or out-of-state.
  • Accomplishments mattered most: Higher ACT/MCA scores and PSEO participation were strongly tied to graduating outside the home region, while heavier CTE engagement (more courses, concentrator/completor status) was associated with staying local.

Overall Interpretation

Across all tiers, the analysis reveals three central themes:

  1. Structural Inequities Persist
    Economic disadvantage, race/ethnicity, and special education status consistently constrain postsecondary success. These students are less likely to enroll immediately, less likely to persist, less likely to graduate, and when they do, they are more likely to complete at lower credential levels and closer to home.

  2. Academic Readiness and PSEO are Game-Changers
    ACT and MCA scores—especially in reading and science—are among the strongest predictors of bachelor’s and graduate attainment, while PSEO participation stands out as the single most effective programmatic lever, doubling the likelihood of bachelor’s+ completion and encouraging geographic mobility.

  3. CTE Pathways Support Local, Sub-Baccalaureate Outcomes
    Intensive CTE engagement aligns strongly with associate-level attainment and local graduation. While CTE provides clear workforce pathways, it appears less effective in propelling students toward bachelor’s or graduate degrees, reflecting its different role in student trajectories.

Conclusion

The analysis underscores that postsecondary pathways are not just about whether students attend, but how, where, and how far they progress.

  • Equity gaps remain entrenched, especially for disadvantaged, racially marginalized, and special education students.
  • Academic preparation and dual-enrollment opportunities (like PSEO) dramatically expand access to higher-level and geographically broader attainment.
  • CTE pathways anchor students locally and at sub-baccalaureate levels, serving as important workforce pipelines but not drivers of higher educational attainment.

Ultimately, policies and practices that strengthen academic readiness, expand access to PSEO, and address demographic inequities will be most effective in raising not only participation and completion rates but also the level and reach of postsecondary success.
