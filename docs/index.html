Pathways After High School – Northeast Minnesota

Purpose of the Research

This research examined which factors most strongly influence whether individuals achieve meaningful employment within Northeast Minnesota’s region (EDR 3) after high school. Using both contingency table analysis (effect sizes, trends over 1, 5, and 10 years) and CART modeling, the goal was to identify which variables most reliably anchor individuals in the regional workforce versus those that push them toward statewide mobility, disconnection, or outmigration.

A special focus was also placed on non-college graduates, who represent a substantial share of the region’s young adults and are at higher risk of disconnection from the labor force. Understanding their pathways provides additional insights into how to build a stronger, more inclusive regional workforce.

The findings provide a roadmap for education and workforce policy, showing how demographics, high school context, accomplishments, and postsecondary experiences interact to shape local labor market attachment.


Summary of Analysis

Analysis 1: Workforce Participation Outcomes and Associated Factors

What This Analysis Examined

This analysis explored how student characteristics, high school experiences, academic performance, and postsecondary engagement are associated with workforce participation outcomes one, five, and ten years after high school graduation in Northeast Minnesota.

Individuals were categorized into one of several outcomes at each time point:

  • Meaningful employment (1,000+ hours)
    • In the same county
    • In the same region
    • In Minnesota outside the region
  • Attending postsecondary
  • Not meaningful employment (with MN record, not attending postsecondary)
  • No Minnesota employment record

Using cross-tabulations, chi-square tests, Cramer’s V, and ANOVA, the analysis examined the strength and direction of relationships between these outcomes and four groups of independent variables:

  • Demographics (e.g., economic disadvantage, Special Education status, race/ethnicity, gender)
  • High school characteristics (e.g., local unemployment, local wages)
  • High school accomplishments (e.g., CTE engagement, ACT, MCA, PSEO, AP, SAT)
  • Postsecondary participation indicators

The purpose was descriptive: to identify which factors most strongly differentiate regional workforce attachment, statewide mobility, and workforce disconnection over time.

Major Findings

1. Two Distinct Pathways Emerge: Academic Mobility vs. Regional Anchoring

The clearest pattern across Years 1, 5, and 10 is a structural split between:

  • College-oriented academic indicators (ACT participation and scores, MCA proficiency, PSEO, AP/SAT)
  • Career and Technical Education (CTE) engagement

Academic and college-prep indicators are strongly associated with postsecondary attendance and, over time, statewide employment. For example, ACT participation shows a very strong Year 1 association (Cramer’s V ≈ 0.36), reflecting a sharp divide between students entering college versus entering direct employment.

In contrast, CTE concentrators/completers and students with heavier CTE course loads are consistently more likely to enter and remain in meaningful regional employment. CTE stands out as the most consistent predictor of regional workforce attachment across time.

2. Special Education Status Is the Most Persistent Risk Indicator

Special Education status consistently differentiates outcomes across all three timeframes.

  • Students receiving Special Education services are more likely to appear in non-meaningful employment or no MN employment record categories.
  • The gap is visible at Year 1 and persists through Year 10.

This pattern indicates that workforce disconnection risk is not random — it is structured and durable.

3. Academic Strength Channels Mobility, Not Regional Retention

Higher MCA scores, ACT scores, and PSEO participation are strongly associated with postsecondary attendance and eventual statewide employment.

The consistent pattern is:

  • Higher academic achievement → postsecondary participation and mobility
  • Lower academic achievement → higher likelihood of direct regional employment or weaker outcomes

Academic achievement expands opportunity, but it does not anchor students to the region.

4. Local Labor Market Conditions Matter — But Modestly

County-level unemployment and wage levels show statistically significant associations, particularly in early years.

However:

  • Effect sizes are generally small.
  • Differences in average wage levels across workforce categories are substantively minimal.
  • Labor market context shapes margins, but does not override educational pathway effects.

Individual preparation and pathway alignment explain more variation than place alone.

5. Postsecondary Participation Is a Structural Divider

Postsecondary attendance is strongly associated with workforce outcomes.

  • Students attending postsecondary are temporarily removed from direct employment categories.
  • Over time, postsecondary exposure is associated with stronger attachment to meaningful employment, especially outside the region.

Even brief exposure to postsecondary education appears to reorganize workforce trajectories.

Overall Interpretation

This analysis reveals that workforce outcomes in Northeast Minnesota are not primarily driven by geography or short-term labor market fluctuations. Instead, they reflect structured educational pathways established during and immediately after high school.

Three patterns stand out:

  1. CTE engagement is the clearest high school-based anchor to sustained regional employment.
  2. Special Education status consistently predicts elevated risk of workforce disconnection.
  3. Academic and college-prep measures channel students toward postsecondary systems and eventual statewide mobility rather than regional retention.

In short, Northeast Minnesota’s workforce attachment patterns reflect dual pathways: a college-mobility pathway driven by academic achievement and early college exposure, and a regional workforce pathway driven by CTE alignment.

This foundational analysis establishes that education pathways—not just local economic conditions—are central to understanding long-term workforce participation in the region.


Analysis 2: What Shapes Postsecondary Pathways?

Why This Analysis Was Conducted

The first analysis showed that postsecondary participation and graduation were among the strongest predictors of sustained workforce participation. Because of that, this second analysis asked a deeper question:

What factors shape who enrolls in postsecondary education, who completes, what credential they earn, and where they graduate?

In other words, if postsecondary attainment strongly influences workforce outcomes, what influences postsecondary attainment itself?

This analysis examined how: - Demographics - High school characteristics - High school accomplishments shape postsecondary pathways across three tiers of outcomes.

Major Findings

1. Demographics Strongly Shape Postsecondary Access and Completion

Across Tier 1 outcomes (enrollment, attendance, and graduation), demographic factors were the most consistent and powerful predictors.

  • Economic disadvantage, Special Education status, and being American Indian, Black, or Hispanic were associated with lower rates of enrollment and graduation.
  • These disparities were not marginal — they were consistent across access and attainment stages.

In short: barriers appear early and persist through completion.

2. Academic Preparation Is a Major Engine of Postsecondary Success

High school academic indicators were among the strongest positive predictors of postsecondary access and completion.

  • ACT and MCA scores strongly differentiated who enrolled and who completed.
  • PSEO participation was particularly powerful — students exposed to college coursework in high school were substantially more likely to enroll and graduate.
  • Academic strength also strongly predicted earning a bachelor’s or higher degree.

Academic readiness and early college exposure clearly open doors.

3. CTE Engagement Reflects a Distinct Postsecondary Pattern — Not a Lack of Attainment

While greater CTE engagement was associated with lower rates of immediate four-year enrollment, the pathway is more nuanced.

  • CTE participants were more likely to earn Associate degrees than bachelor’s degrees.
  • CTE concentrators were more likely to complete postsecondary credentials within the region.
  • CTE engagement was not strongly associated with overall college non-completion — rather, it aligned with shorter-term, applied credentials.

In other words, CTE does not appear to divert students away from postsecondary education entirely. Instead, it aligns students with credential types and institutions that are more closely connected to the regional labor market.

This pattern helps explain why CTE is strongly associated with sustained regional employment in the workforce analysis.

4. Credential Level Is Stratified by Background

When examining highest credential earned:

  • Economic disadvantage and Special Education status were strongly associated with lower likelihood of earning a bachelor’s or higher degree.
  • Academic indicators sharply differentiated who earned higher-level credentials.
  • High school characteristics (local unemployment, wages, rural context) played a comparatively modest role.

Educational attainment differences are structured primarily by preparation and demographic factors — not local economic context alone.

5. Mobility Is Structured by Academic Strength

Tier 3 findings (location of postsecondary graduation) show a clear mobility pattern:

  • Higher-achieving students (higher ACT/MCA scores) were significantly more likely to graduate elsewhere in Minnesota or out of state.
  • Lower-achieving students were more likely to remain in-region.
  • Economically disadvantaged and Special Education students were more likely to graduate locally.
  • Rural graduates were more likely to leave the region for postsecondary completion compared to mixed/urban graduates.

Academic strength is associated not only with college attendance, but with geographic mobility.

Overall Interpretation

This analysis shows that postsecondary pathways in Northeast Minnesota are structured along two distinct but legitimate tracks — not simply “college” versus “no college.”

Three patterns stand out:

  1. Academic preparation is strongly aligned with bachelor’s attainment and geographic mobility. Higher ACT/MCA scores and PSEO participation are associated with enrollment in four-year institutions and graduation outside the region.

  2. CTE engagement is aligned with associate-level credentials and regional institutional completion. CTE concentrators are more likely to earn applied credentials and to graduate from institutions within the region. This suggests CTE pathways are closely tied to local workforce systems.

  3. Demographic disparities shape access to both tracks. Economic disadvantage and Special Education status are associated with lower enrollment and completion across credential types.

Taken together, the findings indicate that postsecondary outcomes in Northeast Minnesota are not random — they reflect structured pathway differentiation established in high school. Academic intensity drives mobility and bachelor’s attainment, while CTE engagement aligns students with regional, applied credentials that feed directly into the local workforce.

Understanding this distinction is essential for regional leaders: strengthening workforce retention is not only about increasing college completion overall, but about understanding which credential pathways are most connected to the region’s economic structure.


Analysis 3: Multivariate Confirmation Analysis – Logistic Regression

(Full Population)

Purpose of This Analysis

This analysis serves as a confirmatory test of the strongest relationships identified in the prior two analyses.

Earlier cross-tabulations showed that:

  • Demographic characteristics,
  • High school preparation and accomplishments (e.g., CTE, ACT, MCA, PSEO),
  • Postsecondary credential depth, and
  • Postsecondary graduation location

were all associated with sustained regional employment.

This multivariate analysis tests whether those relationships remain directionally consistent when key predictors are entered simultaneously into staged logistic regression models.

The goal is confirmatory, not causal.

Outcome Variable

The dependent variable is:

Sustained employment – region

  • 1 = Sustained employment within Northeast Minnesota
  • 0 = All other workforce outcomes (sustained employment outside region, non-sustained employment, no MN employment record)

This mirrors the outcome used in earlier analyses while isolating regional workforce attachment.

Independent Variables

Variables are organized into conceptual blocks:

1. Demographic Variables

  • Race/Ethnicity
  • Economic disadvantage
  • Special Education status
  • Gender

2. High School Preparation and Accomplishments

  • CTE achievement
  • PSEO participation
  • ACT participation
  • MCA Math and Reading

3. Postsecondary Mechanism Variables

  • Highest credential earned
  • Postsecondary graduation location

4. Local Labor Market Context

  • Average wages (relative to state)
  • Average unemployment rate

Modeling Strategy

Predictors are introduced in stages to test attenuation and mechanism strength.

Model 1 – Structural and Preparation Baseline

Includes demographic variables and high school preparation indicators.

Purpose:

  • Confirm whether structural gaps persist net of preparation.
  • Test whether CTE and ACT remain associated with regional attachment after accounting for background.

Model 2 – Add Credential Depth

Adds highest credential earned.

Purpose:

  • Test whether credential stratification explains variation beyond preparation and structure.
  • Evaluate attenuation of demographic and CTE effects once credential level is included.

Model 3 – Add Postsecondary Geography

Adds graduation location.

Purpose:

  • Test whether geography functions as an anchoring mechanism.
  • Evaluate whether credential effects attenuate once location is considered.

Model 4 – Full Specification

Includes credential depth and geography together.

Purpose:

  • Evaluate their independent and combined explanatory power.

Model Fit

Model fit improves progressively across specifications:

  • Model 1 (Preparation + Structure): AIC ≈ 34,286
  • Model 2 (+ Credential Depth): AIC ≈ 33,676
  • Model 3 (+ Geography): AIC ≈ 32,922
  • Model 4 (+ Credential + Geography): AIC ≈ 32,771

The largest improvement occurs when postsecondary geography is introduced, indicating that location of completion explains substantial variation in sustained regional employment.

Confirmed Findings

1. Postsecondary Geography Is the Strongest Independent Predictor

Graduating within the region is the most powerful predictor across specifications:

  • Model 3: OR ≈ 2.42 (~142% higher odds)
  • Model 4: OR ≈ 2.97 (~197% higher odds)

Regional completion functions as the dominant anchoring mechanism.

Graduating outside the region is associated with substantially lower odds of sustained regional employment.

2. Credential Depth Matters, but Attenuates with Geography

Credential level shows a strong association when introduced (Model 2), but its magnitude weakens once graduation location is included.

This indicates that part of the credential effect operates through geography.

3. CTE Remains an Independent Pathway

CTE participation remains positively associated with sustained regional employment across specifications.

Its effect persists net of:

  • Demographics
  • Academic preparation
  • Credential depth
  • Graduation location

This confirms that CTE functions as an independent regional workforce pathway rather than simply reflecting lower academic intensity.

4. Academic Preparation Aligns Modestly with Mobility

ACT participation and higher academic indicators show weaker direct associations with sustained regional employment once postsecondary mechanisms are included.

This aligns with earlier findings that academic intensity is associated more strongly with mobility pathways than with regional anchoring.

5. Structural Disparities Persist

Even in the full specification:

  • Race/ethnicity
  • Economic disadvantage
  • Special Education status

remain associated with differences in sustained regional employment.

Some attenuation occurs when preparation and postsecondary variables are added, but disparities are not fully explained by credential or geography.

What This Analysis Confirms

When tested simultaneously:

  • Regional postsecondary completion is the strongest anchoring mechanism.
  • Credential depth contributes, but geography explains more variation.
  • CTE remains a stable, independent pathway into sustained regional employment.
  • Structural disparities are not eliminated by preparation or attainment alone.

This analysis confirms that the strongest patterns observed in the bivariate analyses remain directionally consistent under multivariate specification.


Analysis 4: Workforce Participation Among Non–Postsecondary Graduates

Purpose of This Analysis

Because postsecondary completion emerged as a dominant predictor of sustained workforce participation in earlier analyses, this analysis isolates a specific subpopulation:

Students who did not complete a postsecondary credential.

The purpose is to examine how demographic characteristics, high school preparation, and high school accomplishments are associated with workforce participation outcomes among individuals whose trajectories are not shaped by credential attainment.

This allows us to evaluate which factors differentiate workforce attachment within the non-college population itself.

Population and Outcome

Study Population: Individuals who did not complete a postsecondary credential.

Workforce Outcomes (1, 5, and 10 years after graduation)

  • Sustained employment – region
  • Sustained employment – MN (outside region)
  • Non-sustained employment
  • No MN employment record

Cross-tabulations and association measures (Cramer’s V, ANOVA) were used to assess differences across characteristics.

Major Findings

1. CTE Remains the Most Consistent Positive Differentiator

Among non-college graduates, CTE engagement continues to differentiate workforce outcomes.

  • CTE concentrators/completers show higher rates of sustained regional employment compared to those with no CTE participation.
  • Greater total CTE coursework is associated with stronger workforce attachment.
  • The association is visible across time points.

This confirms that CTE functions as a workforce pathway even in the absence of postsecondary completion.

2. Special Education Status Continues to Signal Elevated Risk

Special Education status remains strongly associated with weaker workforce attachment.

  • Higher proportions of non-sustained employment and no MN employment record appear among this group.
  • The pattern persists beyond Year 1.

This suggests that risk factors identified in the full population remain concentrated within the non-college subgroup.

3. Academic Preparation Shows Graded but Modest Associations

Academic indicators (ACT participation, MCA proficiency):

  • Continue to differentiate workforce outcomes,
  • But effect sizes are generally smaller than in the full population.

Without postsecondary completion, academic strength does not reorganize outcomes as dramatically, though it still signals differential attachment.

4. Economic Disadvantage Remains Structurally Relevant

Economic disadvantage is associated with higher rates of weaker workforce attachment within the non-college population.

The relationship is directionally consistent with earlier analyses, though magnitude varies across time.

5. Local Labor Market Context Plays a Limited Role

County-level unemployment and wage variables show relatively small associations.

As in earlier analyses, labor market context appears secondary to individual preparation and pathway alignment.

What This Analysis Demonstrates

Within the non-college population:

  • CTE engagement is the clearest high school-based lever associated with sustained regional employment.
  • Special Education status and economic disadvantage remain associated with weaker workforce attachment.
  • Academic preparation matters, but does not substitute for credential attainment.
  • Local economic conditions do not fully explain workforce differences.

Importantly, this analysis confirms that even when postsecondary completion is removed from the equation, workforce outcomes remain structured by preparation and demographic factors.

The mechanisms shaping workforce participation among non-college graduates are not random; they reflect earlier pathway differentiation established in high school.


Analysis 5: Persistent Non-Meaningful Workforce Participation Among Non–Postsecondary Graduates

Why This Analysis Was Conducted

The prior analysis of non–postsecondary graduates showed that workforce outcomes within this group were structured by CTE participation, academic preparation, and demographic characteristics.

However, that analysis examined workforce outcomes at individual time points (Year 1, Year 5, Year 10). It did not distinguish between:

  • Individuals who temporarily experienced non-meaningful employment, and
  • Individuals who persistently failed to achieve sustained employment across multiple years.

This analysis isolates a smaller, higher-risk subgroup:

Non–postsecondary graduates who repeatedly appear in the non-meaningful employment category across observed years.

The goal is to understand whether persistent workforce instability is random within the non-college population, or whether it clusters around identifiable characteristics.

Defining Persistence

The analysis categorizes non–postsecondary graduates based on whether they:

  • Achieved sustained employment at any observed time point, or
  • Persistently remained in non-meaningful employment categories across multiple years.

This moves from a cross-sectional perspective to a longitudinal stability lens.

Major Findings

1. Persistent Non-Meaningful Employment Is Not Random

The subgroup of individuals who repeatedly fail to reach sustained employment is not evenly distributed across characteristics.

Persistent instability is concentrated among:

  • Students who received Special Education services
  • Students identified as economically disadvantaged
  • Students with weaker academic preparation indicators
  • Students with little or no CTE engagement

The clustering of these characteristics indicates compounding risk rather than isolated short-term transitions.

2. CTE Engagement Differentiates Persistence

Among non–postsecondary graduates:

  • Those with greater CTE concentration are less likely to appear in the persistently non-meaningful employment category.
  • Individuals with no CTE participation are overrepresented among persistently unstable workforce participants.

This reinforces earlier findings that CTE functions as a stabilizing workforce pathway even in the absence of postsecondary completion.

3. Academic Preparation Still Signals Risk, Though Modestly

Lower MCA proficiency and absence of ACT participation are more common among persistently unstable individuals.

However, academic indicators alone do not fully explain persistent instability.

This suggests that academic preparation interacts with other structural factors rather than operating independently.

4. Economic Disadvantage and Special Education Status Compound Risk

The most consistent predictors of persistent non-meaningful employment are:

  • Special Education status
  • Economic disadvantage

These groups show disproportionately high representation among individuals who never achieve sustained employment across observed years.

The persistence of these patterns suggests that workforce instability is not merely transitional — for some individuals, it becomes entrenched.

What This Analysis Adds

This analysis shifts the focus from “who is not meaningfully employed at a given time” to:

Who remains disconnected from sustained employment over time?

It shows that within the non-college population:

  • A subset experiences persistent workforce instability.
  • That instability is strongly associated with structural and preparation-based factors.
  • CTE participation appears to mitigate, though not eliminate, risk.

Importantly, this analysis highlights a population for whom traditional workforce entry pathways are not producing durable attachment.

For regional leaders, this identifies where targeted intervention, transition supports, and employer engagement may be most necessary — not broadly across all non-college graduates, but among those exhibiting sustained disconnection patterns.


Analysis 6: Multivariate Confirmation – Non–Postsecondary Graduates

Purpose of This Analysis

The previous non–postsecondary analyses showed that workforce outcomes within this subgroup were associated with:

  • CTE participation
  • Academic preparation indicators
  • Special Education status
  • Economic disadvantage
  • Local labor market context

This final analysis tests whether those relationships remain statistically significant when considered simultaneously within the non-college population.

Unlike the full-population multivariate model, postsecondary credential and geography variables are excluded by design, since this subgroup did not complete a credential. The focus here is to confirm which pre-graduation characteristics independently differentiate sustained workforce participation among non-college graduates.

The modeling strategy mirrors the staged confirmation used earlier.

Outcome Variable

For each time point (Year 1, Year 5, Year 10):

Sustained employment – region

  • 1 = Sustained employment within Northeast Minnesota
  • 0 = All other workforce outcomes

Separate logistic regression models are estimated for each year.

Modeling Strategy

Predictors are introduced in stages:

Model 1 – Structural and Preparation Baseline

Includes:

  • Race/Ethnicity
  • Economic disadvantage
  • Special Education status
  • Gender
  • Academic indicators (MCA, ACT participation)

Purpose:

  • Confirm whether structural disparities persist within the non-college population.
  • Evaluate whether academic preparation differentiates regional attachment.

Model 2 – Add High School Accomplishments

Adds:

  • CTE achievement
  • Other high school engagement variables

Purpose:

  • Test whether CTE remains independently associated with sustained regional employment.
  • Assess attenuation of demographic effects once pathway participation is included.

Model 3 – Add Local Labor Market Context

Adds:

  • Average unemployment rate
  • Average wage level

Purpose:

  • Evaluate whether local economic conditions independently shape workforce attachment among non-college graduates.

Major Findings

1. CTE Remains the Most Consistent Independent Predictor

Across years and model specifications:

  • CTE participation remains positively associated with sustained regional employment.
  • The effect persists after controlling for demographics and academic preparation.

This confirms that CTE operates as an independent stabilizing pathway within the non-college population.

2. Special Education Status Remains a Strong Risk Indicator

Special Education status continues to show negative associations with sustained regional employment across specifications.

The relationship attenuates slightly once high school accomplishments are introduced but remains statistically meaningful.

This confirms that structural vulnerability persists even within the non-college subgroup.

3. Academic Preparation Shows Modest and Inconsistent Effects

Academic indicators (MCA proficiency, ACT participation):

  • Show smaller and less consistent effects compared to CTE.
  • In some years, lose statistical significance once CTE and structural variables are included.

This suggests that, absent credential attainment, academic strength alone does not strongly differentiate long-term regional workforce attachment.

4. Economic Disadvantage Retains Influence

Economic disadvantage remains associated with lower odds of sustained regional employment, though magnitude varies across time points.

Attenuation occurs when pathway participation is included, indicating partial mediation through high school experiences.

5. Local Labor Market Context Has Limited Independent Effect

When unemployment and wage variables are added:

  • Effects are generally small.
  • Model improvement is modest relative to structural and pathway variables.

This reinforces earlier findings that individual preparation and pathway engagement explain more variation than local economic conditions alone.

What This Analysis Confirms

Within the non-college population:

  • CTE participation remains the strongest independent high school-based predictor of sustained regional employment.
  • Special Education status and economic disadvantage consistently signal elevated risk.
  • Academic preparation alone does not strongly reorganize outcomes absent postsecondary attainment.
  • Local labor market conditions play a secondary role.

This final confirmation analysis demonstrates that even when credential attainment is removed from the equation, workforce attachment among non-college graduates is structured and predictable based on preparation pathways and demographic factors.


The Story

Thesis

Northeast Minnesota graduates follow distinct educational and workforce pathways shaped by preparation, credential type, and geography — and regional workforce retention is most strongly linked to local postsecondary completion and CTE alignment, while academic intensity is associated with broader geographic mobility.

1. Where Do Northeast Minnesota Graduates Go After High School?

Before examining what shapes workforce outcomes, it is important to understand what actually happens to graduates in the years following high school. Using linked education and employment records, this analysis tracks graduates from Northeast Minnesota at one, five, and ten years after graduation.

Workforce outcomes are categorized as:

  • Sustained employment – Northeast (1,000+ hours with a single employer in the region)
  • Sustained employment – Minnesota (outside the region)
  • Non-sustained employment (employment record, but below the sustained threshold)
  • No Minnesota employment record
  • Attending postsecondary (in early years)

Examining outcomes across time reveals three broad patterns.

1.1 Year 1: Immediate Sorting After Graduation

One year after high school, graduates are distributed across three primary statuses:

  1. Entering the workforce directly
  2. Enrolling in postsecondary education
  3. Experiencing weak or no workforce attachment

At this stage, a substantial share of students are enrolled in postsecondary institutions and therefore are not yet fully participating in the workforce. Among those working, some are already achieving sustained employment in the region, while others are either working outside the region or in non-sustained positions.

Year 1 reflects initial sorting rather than long-term attachment.

1.2 Year 5: Divergence Becomes Clear

By Year 5, clearer patterns emerge.

  • Many students who enrolled in postsecondary have completed credentials and transitioned into the workforce.
  • Sustained employment—both in the region and elsewhere in Minnesota—becomes more stable and distinguishable.
  • A subset of individuals continues to experience non-sustained employment or no Minnesota employment record.

This period marks the first clear divergence between those who have established durable workforce attachment and those who have not.

1.3 Year 10: Stability and Persistence

Ten years after graduation, workforce outcomes largely stabilize.

Three durable patterns are visible:

  • A group that is consistently engaged in sustained regional employment.
  • A group that is stably employed outside the region.
  • A smaller but significant group that remains in non-sustained employment or without a Minnesota employment record.

By Year 10, these patterns are not short-term transitions—they represent sustained trajectories.

1.4 What This Tells Us

The data show that graduates from Northeast Minnesota do not follow a single pathway. Instead:

  • Some establish long-term careers within the region.
  • Some pursue postsecondary education and ultimately build careers elsewhere.
  • Some struggle to achieve sustained workforce attachment.

Importantly, these trajectories begin forming early but solidify over time. Understanding what influences these transition points is critical for regional leaders who are concerned with workforce retention, economic vitality, and opportunity for young adults.

The next section examines the two major transition points that shape whether graduates remain anchored to the region or move into broader mobility pathways.


2. The Two Transition Points That Shape Regional Workforce Outcomes

The earlier section described where graduates are positioned at Years 1, 5, and 10. This section focuses on the two key transition points that most strongly shape whether individuals ultimately become anchored to the Northeast workforce or pursue careers elsewhere.

These transition points are not abstract concepts—they are directly supported by the descriptive and multivariate analyses.

2.1 Transition Point One: Immediate Postsecondary Entry vs. Direct Workforce Entry

The first major sorting moment occurs in the year immediately following high school graduation.

Graduates generally move into one of two directions:

  • Postsecondary enrollment, or
  • Direct entry into the workforce.

What Differentiates These Paths?

The analyses show that:

  • Academic preparation indicators (ACT participation and performance, MCA proficiency, PSEO participation) are strongly associated with postsecondary enrollment and eventual credential completion.
  • Students with stronger academic preparation are more likely to pursue bachelor’s degrees and enroll in institutions outside the region.
  • Students with heavier CTE engagement are more likely to enter the workforce directly or pursue shorter-term credentials.

This first transition point reflects differences in preparation and exposure during high school. It is not inherently about staying or leaving the region—it is about entering distinct educational or workforce pathways.

At this stage, geographic outcomes are not yet fixed.

2.2 Transition Point Two: Postsecondary Completion – Credential Type and Location

The second and more consequential transition point occurs at postsecondary completion.

The multivariate confirmation analysis shows that:

  • Graduation location is the strongest independent predictor of sustained regional employment.
  • Completing postsecondary education within Northeast Minnesota substantially increases the likelihood of sustained employment in the region.
  • Graduating elsewhere in Minnesota or out of state is associated with lower likelihood of returning for sustained regional employment.
  • Credential depth matters, but its independent effect attenuates once graduation location is considered.

This finding reshapes a common narrative.

Academic strength does not automatically lead to leaving the region. High-achieving students who complete postsecondary education within Northeast Minnesota are strongly likely to remain employed in the region.

Geography functions as an anchoring mechanism.

2.3 The Role of CTE Across Both Transition Points

CTE engagement plays a role at both transition stages:

  • It is associated with sustained regional employment among students who enter the workforce directly.
  • It is associated with earning Associate degrees.
  • It is associated with completing postsecondary credentials within the region.
  • It remains a significant predictor of regional workforce attachment even when controlling for academic preparation and demographic characteristics.

This suggests that CTE operates not simply as an alternative to college, but as a regional alignment mechanism that increases the probability of remaining connected to local institutions and employers.

2.4 What This Means for Regional Retention

Regional workforce attachment is shaped less by whether students pursue higher education and more by:

  • Where they complete it, and
  • Whether their high school preparation aligned them with local opportunity structures.

The evidence does not support a simplistic “high achievers leave” narrative. Instead, it shows:

  • Academic preparation expands options.
  • Regional postsecondary completion anchors talent.
  • CTE strengthens alignment between education and local labor market demand.

The next section examines which specific characteristics are most strongly associated with sustained regional workforce participation once these transition points are considered.


3. What Is Associated with Sustained Workforce Participation in the Region?

Having identified the two major transition points that shape workforce trajectories, this section focuses directly on the central question for regional leaders:

Which characteristics are most consistently associated with sustained employment in Northeast Minnesota?

The findings below draw from both descriptive and multivariate confirmation analyses.

Importantly, these are associations—not causal claims—but the consistency across models strengthens confidence that these relationships are meaningful.

3.1 Regional Postsecondary Completion Is the Strongest Anchor

Across multivariate models, the single strongest predictor of sustained regional employment is:

Completing postsecondary education within Northeast Minnesota.

Graduates who complete a credential in-region are substantially more likely to achieve sustained employment in the region compared to those who graduate elsewhere.

This effect persists even after controlling for:

  • Academic preparation
  • CTE participation
  • Credential level
  • Demographic characteristics
  • Local labor market conditions

Credential level matters, but geography explains more variation in regional retention than degree depth alone.

For regional leaders, this suggests that local institutions are not simply educational providers—they function as anchoring infrastructure for long-term workforce attachment.

3.2 CTE Engagement Is a Consistent Regional Alignment Mechanism

CTE participation emerges as one of the most stable high school-based predictors of sustained regional employment.

CTE is associated with:

  • Higher likelihood of sustained regional employment in the full population.
  • Associate degree attainment.
  • Regional postsecondary graduation.
  • Reduced likelihood of persistent non-meaningful employment among non-college graduates.

Importantly, CTE remains positively associated with regional workforce attachment even when academic preparation and demographic characteristics are controlled.

This indicates that CTE is not simply a proxy for lower academic intensity. It functions as a regional workforce alignment mechanism that increases the probability of durable local employment.

3.3 Academic Preparation Expands Options—But Does Not Predetermine Location

Academic indicators such as ACT participation, MCA proficiency, and PSEO participation are strongly associated with:

  • Postsecondary enrollment
  • Bachelor’s attainment
  • Broader geographic mobility

However, once postsecondary graduation location is included in multivariate models, academic preparation shows weaker direct association with sustained regional employment.

This suggests that academic preparation expands opportunity structures. Whether that opportunity translates into regional retention depends heavily on where postsecondary education is completed.

High academic achievement does not inherently lead to regional exit. Regional completion mediates that relationship.

3.4 Local Labor Market Conditions Play a Secondary Role

County-level unemployment and wage measures show statistically detectable but comparatively modest associations with sustained regional employment.

When educational pathways and preparation variables are included in models, labor market context explains less variation than:

  • Postsecondary geography
  • CTE participation
  • Demographic factors

This does not mean economic conditions are irrelevant. It means that education-to-workforce alignment appears to shape long-term attachment more strongly than short-term labor market fluctuations.

3.5 Structural Disparities Persist

Across both full-population and non-college analyses:

  • Special Education status
  • Economic disadvantage
  • Certain racial/ethnic groups

are consistently associated with lower rates of sustained regional employment.

While some of these relationships attenuate when preparation and postsecondary variables are included, disparities are not fully explained by credential attainment or geography.

This indicates that workforce instability for some groups reflects broader structural barriers rather than temporary transitions.

3.6 Summary of Anchoring Factors

When considered together, the strongest associations with sustained regional workforce participation are:

  1. Regional postsecondary completion
  2. CTE engagement
  3. Absence of structural vulnerability factors

Academic preparation plays an important role in shaping pathways, but geography and alignment appear to be more decisive in determining long-term regional attachment.

The next section turns specifically to the subgroup of graduates who experience persistent workforce instability and examines what differentiates them.


4. Who Experiences Persistent Workforce Instability?

While many graduates establish stable careers—either within Northeast Minnesota or elsewhere—a smaller but important subgroup experiences repeated difficulty achieving sustained employment.

This section focuses on that subgroup.

Rather than examining workforce status at a single point in time, the analysis identifies non–postsecondary graduates who persistently appear in non-sustained employment categories across multiple years. This shifts the lens from short-term transition challenges to long-term instability.

4.1 Persistent Instability Is Not Random

The deeper non-college analysis shows that repeated non-meaningful employment is not evenly distributed across the population.

Persistent instability clusters around identifiable characteristics, including:

  • Special Education status
  • Economic disadvantage
  • Lower academic preparation
  • Limited or no CTE engagement

These patterns are consistent across Years 1, 5, and 10.

This suggests that workforce instability is not simply a temporary phase for everyone. For some individuals, it becomes entrenched.

4.2 CTE Participation Differentiates Stability

Among non–postsecondary graduates:

  • Individuals with CTE concentration are less likely to experience persistent non-meaningful employment.
  • Those without CTE engagement are overrepresented among persistently unstable workforce participants.

This reinforces earlier findings that CTE functions as a stabilizing pathway—not only increasing the likelihood of sustained regional employment, but also reducing the probability of long-term instability.

4.3 Academic Preparation Alone Does Not Prevent Instability

Lower MCA proficiency and lack of ACT participation are more common among persistently unstable individuals.

However, academic indicators alone do not fully explain persistent instability once demographic and pathway variables are considered.

This suggests that academic preparation interacts with structural factors rather than operating independently as a protective factor.

4.4 Structural Vulnerability Compounds Over Time

Special Education status and economic disadvantage consistently show elevated representation among persistently unstable individuals.

Importantly, these disparities do not disappear as graduates age. By Year 10, persistent non-meaningful employment is still concentrated among these groups.

This indicates that early barriers can translate into long-term workforce vulnerability without targeted intervention.

4.5 What This Means for Regional Leaders

The majority of graduates eventually establish stable workforce attachment. However, a smaller subgroup remains disconnected over time.

For regional leaders, this suggests two complementary priorities:

  1. Strengthening alignment pathways (CTE, regional postsecondary completion) that increase the likelihood of sustained attachment.
  2. Designing targeted supports for graduates at highest risk of persistent instability—particularly those facing structural vulnerabilities.

Persistent workforce instability represents both an economic and community challenge. The evidence indicates that it is patterned and predictable, which means it is also potentially addressable.

The final section synthesizes what these findings collectively imply for education systems, workforce development, and regional strategy.


5. What This Means for Northeast Minnesota

This report set out to answer a practical question:

Where do Northeast Minnesota’s high school graduates go after graduation, and what appears to be associated with sustained workforce participation in the region?

Across six analyses—descriptive, subgroup, and multivariate confirmation—a coherent pattern emerges. Workforce outcomes are structured around preparation, credential pathways, geography, and structural vulnerability.

The implications below are grounded in those findings.

5.1 Regional Postsecondary Institutions Function as Anchors

The strongest and most consistent predictor of sustained regional employment is:

Completing postsecondary education within Northeast Minnesota.

Graduation location explains more variation in regional retention than credential depth alone.

This suggests that regional colleges and technical institutions are not simply education providers—they are workforce anchoring institutions. Their role extends beyond instruction to long-term talent retention.

Implication for leaders: Strengthening regional postsecondary capacity, visibility, and employer alignment may be one of the most direct strategies for improving long-term workforce retention.

5.2 CTE Is a Workforce Alignment Mechanism

CTE engagement consistently shows positive associations with:

  • Sustained regional employment
  • Associate credential attainment
  • Regional postsecondary graduation
  • Reduced persistent instability among non-college graduates

CTE is not merely an alternative to college. It appears to function as a bridge between high school, local postsecondary institutions, and regional employers.

Implication for leaders: Investments in high-quality, industry-aligned CTE pathways—especially those connected to regional institutions—may strengthen both immediate workforce entry and longer-term retention.

5.3 Academic Preparation Expands Opportunity—But Geography Shapes Retention

Academic indicators (ACT, MCA, PSEO) are strongly associated with postsecondary enrollment and bachelor’s attainment.

However:

  • Academic strength alone does not determine whether a graduate stays in or leaves the region.
  • Regional completion significantly increases the likelihood of remaining in the region—even among high-achieving students.

The data do not support a simplistic “high achievers leave” narrative. Rather:

Academic preparation expands options. Regional completion anchors talent.

Implication for leaders: Efforts to retain talent should focus less on limiting mobility and more on strengthening local opportunity structures that make regional completion and employment attractive and viable options.

5.4 Persistent Instability Is Concentrated and Predictable

A subset of graduates—particularly non–postsecondary completers—experience repeated non-sustained employment.

Persistent instability is associated with:

  • Special Education status
  • Economic disadvantage
  • Lower academic preparation
  • Limited CTE engagement

These patterns are durable across time.

Implication for leaders: Targeted transition supports, employer partnerships, and wraparound services for high-risk students may reduce long-term workforce disconnection.

5.5 Education and Workforce Systems Are Interdependent

Across analyses, local labor market conditions explain less variation in sustained regional employment than educational pathways and geography.

This suggests that workforce retention is not solely an economic development issue or solely an education issue.

It is a systems alignment issue.

Regional workforce stability appears strongest when:

  • High school pathways align with regional postsecondary institutions,
  • Postsecondary completion occurs within the region, and
  • Employers are positioned to absorb credentialed graduates into sustained employment.

5.6 A Balanced Regional Strategy

The evidence suggests three complementary priorities:

  1. Strengthen regional postsecondary completion as a retention anchor.
  2. Expand and align CTE pathways with regional industry demand.
  3. Provide targeted supports for students facing structural barriers to stable employment.

Importantly, these priorities are not mutually exclusive. They operate at different points in the pathway system.

Concluding Perspective

Northeast Minnesota graduates follow structured and predictable educational and workforce pathways.

Sustained regional workforce participation is most strongly associated with:

  • Completing postsecondary education within the region,
  • Engaging in career-aligned high school pathways,
  • And avoiding compounded structural vulnerabilities.

Retention is not solely about preventing mobility. It is about strengthening anchors.

The findings in this report suggest that when preparation, credential pathways, and regional opportunity structures are aligned, long-term workforce attachment within Northeast Minnesota is both achievable and durable.
