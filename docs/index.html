Pathways After High School – Northeast Minnesota

Pathways to Meaningful Regional Employment in Northeast Minnesota

Purpose of the Research

This research examined which factors most strongly influence whether individuals achieve meaningful employment within Northeast Minnesota’s region (EDR 3) after high school. Using both contingency table analysis (effect sizes, trends over 1, 5, and 10 years) and CART modeling, the goal was to identify which variables most reliably anchor individuals in the regional workforce versus those that push them toward statewide mobility, disconnection, or outmigration.

A special focus was also placed on non-college graduates, who represent a substantial share of the region’s young adults and are at higher risk of disconnection from the labor force. Understanding their pathways provides additional insights into how to build a stronger, more inclusive regional workforce.

The findings provide a roadmap for education and workforce policy, showing how demographics, high school context, accomplishments, and postsecondary experiences interact to shape local labor market attachment.

Contingency Table Insights

Demographics

Demographic factors were relatively modest predictors overall. Special Education status showed the strongest short-term influence (V = 0.258, Yr 1), while Race/Ethnicity maintained moderate effects over time. Most demographic effects faded by 10 years, suggesting that institutional and educational pathways, not demographics alone, determine long-run anchoring.

For non-college graduates specifically, demographics had a sharper impact. SpecialEd students were persistently less likely to secure meaningful regional work and more likely to face disconnection. Economic status shaped outcomes differently: lower-income students were more region-bound but faced higher risks of unstable work, while higher-income peers more often pursued statewide opportunities.

High School Characteristics

Local economic context strongly influenced early outcomes. Unemployment rates (F = 53.1, Yr 1) and wage levels shaped whether graduates found local work or disconnected. Rural graduates were somewhat more likely to remain in meaningful regional jobs, while peers in urban or mixed settings pursued statewide employment. By 10 years, these contextual effects weakened, giving way to individual educational accomplishments.

High School Accomplishments

Clear patterns emerged between academic and technical preparation:
- Academic indicators (ACT, MCA, PSEO, AP) predicted statewide mobility and non-regional pathways.
- CTE achievement and course-taking were the strongest predictors of regional meaningful employment, with MCA Math showing the largest single predictive power overall (F = 1344.5, Yr 1).

For non-college graduates, CTE engagement was the most reliable pathway into meaningful regional employment, persisting through 10 years. By contrast, higher academic scorers and PSEO participants were less likely to anchor regionally, instead moving into statewide or out-of-region opportunities.

Postsecondary Experiences

For the full population, postsecondary experiences were the most powerful predictors overall:
- Attendance and completion of postsecondary education separated stable from disconnected pathways.
- Credential level and location nuanced outcomes:
- Regional/2-year credentials anchored locally,
- 4-year/out-of-state credentials expanded mobility,
- Non-completers were most at risk of poor outcomes.

Non-college graduates, by definition, did not experience these pathways, making their employment outcomes far more dependent on high school accomplishments and local economic context.

CART Analysis Findings

CART models confirmed and sharpened the contingency table results.

Anchors of Regional Employment

  1. Regional Postsecondary Completion anchored the broader graduate population long-term.
  2. CTE Engagement mattered across both groups. Two or more CTE courses increased Year 1 anchoring, while concentrators/completers sustained regional employment through Year 10 — even for non-college grads.
  3. Moderate Academic Readiness (ACT participation, MCA proficiency) anchored students regionally when paired with CTE or regional postsecondary experiences.

Persistent Barriers

  • Failure to complete postsecondary was the strongest barrier overall, though irrelevant for non-college grads.
  • SpecialEd needs consistently predicted disconnection in both groups.
  • Weak academics blocked employability and increased risk of unstable or non-meaningful work.
  • Unfavorable local economies (low wages, high unemployment) amplified disconnection risks, especially for non-college grads.

Postsecondary Pathways: Credential vs. Location

For students who attended postsecondary:
- Graduation Location determined whether graduates stayed rooted in the region. Regional institutions consistently anchored graduates, particularly disadvantaged but academically strong students.
- Highest Credential Level determined how far opportunities extended. Sub-baccalaureate degrees aligned closely with regional jobs, while bachelor’s and higher opened broader mobility. Non-completers faced the weakest outcomes.

For non-college graduates, these dynamics were absent. Their pathways were instead shaped by CTE participation, high school academics, and local economic conditions. CTE provided the anchoring force that postsecondary completion otherwise plays.

Non-College Graduate Insights

Relationships Analysis

  • CTE engagement was the strongest and most consistent predictor of meaningful regional work.
  • Lower ACT/MCA scorers were more likely to enter regional jobs, while higher scorers pursued broader opportunities.
  • SpecialEd students faced persistent disadvantages, rarely securing regional work.
  • Economic status showed mixed effects: lower-income grads were more region-bound but also more likely to disconnect.
  • Local context mattered: rural settings and stronger county economies improved anchoring.

Predictive Analysis

  • Year 1 models provided the clearest separation; predictive power declined over time.
  • CTE engagement remained the most durable anchor.
  • SpecialEd status was the most consistent barrier to connection.
  • Academic strength (especially ACT participation and scores) acted as mobility markers, steering students toward statewide jobs.
  • Local wages/unemployment set the broader context, with stronger labor markets supporting regional employment.

Overall, non-college graduates anchored regionally through CTE and favorable local economies, while academically strong students were more mobile and SpecialEd students faced long-term risks of disconnection.

Synthesis and Policy Implications

The findings converge on a dual pathway model for Northeast Minnesota:

  1. CTE + Regional Postsecondary Completion → Anchoring Pathway
    • Anchors both college-goers and non-college grads in meaningful regional jobs.
    • Particularly effective for students with moderate academic preparation.
  2. Academic + 4-Year/Out-of-State Postsecondary → Mobility Pathway
    • Expands opportunities statewide and nationally.
    • Often results in outmigration but yields higher credentials.

For non-college graduates, CTE fills the role that postsecondary completion otherwise plays in anchoring young adults. Strong local economies further enhance this effect, while weak academics or SpecialEd needs elevate disconnection risks.

Policy priorities include:

  • Strengthening regional postsecondary pipelines, with focus on completion.
  • Expanding and deepening CTE pathways, ensuring students progress to concentrator/completer status.
  • Supporting moderate academic readiness to ensure workforce-ready skills for both college and non-college pathways.
  • Providing targeted interventions for SpecialEd students and academically weaker groups.
  • Investing in local wage growth and employment opportunities to prevent disconnection among non-college graduates.

Conclusion

Meaningful regional employment in Northeast Minnesota is anchored by CTE engagement, moderate academic readiness, and regional postsecondary completion. For non-college graduates, CTE is the single most important pathway, substituting for the anchoring effect of college completion.

At the same time, the region must support the mobility pathway: academically strong students will continue to pursue broader opportunities. Balancing these pathways — anchoring some while supporting mobility for others — is essential for long-term workforce resilience.

The most dependable strategies are therefore:

  • Ensuring students engage deeply in CTE,
  • Supporting regional postsecondary completion where possible, and
  • Creating inclusive supports for SpecialEd students and academically weaker graduates to prevent long-term disconnection.

Predictive employment CART Analysis

Employment Outcomes

This analysis examined what factors anchor young adults into meaningful employment in their high school region versus drifting into three alternative paths:
1. Employment elsewhere in Minnesota
2. Not meaningful employment/not attending postsecondary
3. No Minnesota employment record/not attending postsecondary

Using CART models at 1, 5, and 10 years after high school, the goal was to identify the most influential variables and extract actionable insights for regional workforce and education policy.

Key Anchors of Regional Employment

Across all models, three consistent anchors emerged:

  1. Regional Postsecondary Completion
    • By 5 and 10 years, the strongest predictor of staying anchored in the region is graduating from a postsecondary institution within the region.
    • Higher credential levels (associate and bachelor’s degrees) amplify this effect.
  2. CTE Engagement
    • In high school, simply taking 2+ CTE courses increases the likelihood of regional employment (especially by Year 1).
    • By Year 10, CTE concentrators and completers show significantly higher rates of remaining in the region, even when academic scores are modest.
  3. Moderate-to-Strong Academic Readiness
    • ACT participation and moderate ACT scores (not necessarily the highest) anchor students in the region early on.
    • MCA Math and Reading proficiency sustain attachment at later stages, providing a foundation for both CTE and postsecondary success.

Persistent Barriers

  1. Failure to Complete Postsecondary
    • Students who do not graduate from any postsecondary program are consistently at higher risk of disconnection or leaving the region.
  2. Weak Academic Preparation
    • Very low ACT scores and MCA performance are early barriers, limiting postsecondary access and undermining employability.
  3. Limited CTE Engagement
    • Students without depth in CTE (no concentration or completion) are more likely to fall into non-meaningful employment or lack a Minnesota employment record.
  4. Economic and Demographic Context
    • Low local wages and higher unemployment rates increase the risk of disconnection.
    • White students with weak academic or CTE preparation were more likely to leave or disconnect, while non-White students paired with CTE/PS pathways showed stronger anchoring.
    • Special Education status appears as a short-term barrier in Year 1, but its influence diminishes once postsecondary pathways are engaged.

Policy Implications for Northeast Minnesota

The modeling results point to clear levers for strengthening regional workforce attachment:

  1. Expand Regional Postsecondary Pipelines
    • Strengthen partnerships between high schools and regional colleges/universities.
    • Provide targeted supports to ensure not just access but completion of credentials in the region.
  2. Deepen CTE Pathways
    • Encourage students to progress from introductory courses to CTE concentrator/completer status, which has strong long-term anchoring effects.
    • Align CTE pathways tightly with regional industries and labor market needs.
  3. Support Academic Readiness at Moderate Levels
    • Ensure broad ACT participation and boost MCA Math/Reading performance.
    • Focus less on elite performance and more on raising the floor to “workforce-ready” levels.
  4. Mitigate Barriers for At-Risk Groups
    • Target interventions for students with low academic scores, no early CTE exposure, or no clear postsecondary plan.
    • Provide wage incentives and local internships to keep academically mobile students from leaving the region.

Conclusion

The results show that anchoring students in Northeast Minnesota’s workforce is not about producing the highest scorers or sending students to selective institutions elsewhere. Instead, the most powerful strategies are:

  • Ensuring students complete postsecondary programs within the region,
  • Building strong career and technical pathways, and
  • Raising academic preparation to moderate workforce-ready levels.

Barriers remain significant for those who leave postsecondary incomplete, underperform academically, or fail to engage in CTE. Addressing these gaps offers the most direct path to strengthening regional workforce resilience.


Relationships Post-secondary Pathways

Comparison of Postsecondary Graduation Location and Highest Credential Earned

Both postsecondary graduation location and highest credential earned emerged as powerful predictors of meaningful employment, but they influenced outcomes in different ways.

Graduation location primarily reflected where students were most likely to remain employed. The CART analysis showed that students who graduated from a regional institution were the most likely to secure meaningful local employment, demonstrating the strong anchoring effect of nearby colleges. By contrast, students graduating outside the region, especially outside Minnesota, were less likely to stay in the regional labor market. Academic readiness, particularly higher ACT scores, increased the likelihood of completing a regional credential, with disadvantaged students who scored well on the ACT especially anchored to local institutions.

Highest credential earned, on the other hand, shaped how far students’ employment opportunities extended. Students who attained a bachelor’s degree or higher were less likely to remain employed locally, as these credentials opened access to broader state and national labor markets. In contrast, students with associate degrees or lower were more likely to stay in regional employment, reflecting a closer alignment between sub-baccalaureate training and local job structures. Non-completers were at the highest risk of poor employment outcomes altogether, with many remaining in the region but in lower-skill, less stable jobs.

Summary and Policy Implications

Together, the two models highlight a complementary dynamic: graduation location determines whether students stay rooted in the region, while credential level determines whether they pursue local opportunities or broader labor markets. Regional institutions play a critical role in anchoring students, particularly those from disadvantaged backgrounds with strong academic preparation, while credential level shapes the degree of geographic mobility.

For policy and planning, this suggests two priorities. First, strengthening regional postsecondary institutions can enhance local workforce retention, especially by supporting high-achieving but economically constrained students who might otherwise leave. Second, expanding opportunities for sub-baccalaureate credentials can help align education with regional labor market needs, while creating clearer pathways for bachelor’s graduates to find meaningful employment locally may help reduce outmigration. In short, location ties students to place, while credential level influences the reach of their opportunities—a balance that policymakers and institutions can shape through program design and workforce partnerships.


Predictive post-secondary pathways CART

Overall Model Performance

Across both dependent variables — postsecondary graduation location and highest credential earned — the pre-pruned CART models achieved accuracies of 61.7% and 64.2%, respectively. Both represented clear improvements over baseline distributions and showed that a relatively small set of predictors consistently explains meaningful variation in student pathways.

While not capturing all individual-level complexity, these models highlight distinct sets of academic, demographic, and experiential variables that shape whether students remain anchored in the Southern MN workforce pipeline or pursue mobility pathways.

Key Predictors Across Models

The following predictors emerged as the most consistently influential:

  • Academic readiness: MCA Reading and ACT Composite Score.
  • Economic status: Persistent disadvantages for low-income students.
  • Race/Ethnicity: White and Asian/PI students more likely to reach higher credentials; Black, AI, and Hispanic students more often concentrated in lower or incomplete outcomes.
  • PSEO participation: Steers students toward bachelor’s+ and out-of-region completions.
  • CTE engagement (courses taken): Reinforces regional and sub-baccalaureate pathways.

These predictors operate in two clear pipelines — one driving local attachment through sub-baccalaureate credentials, and one fostering mobility through advanced credentials.

Interpretation of Pathways

1. Graduation Location (ps.grad.location)

  • Regional completions clustered among economically disadvantaged students with moderate academic readiness.
  • Non-completion was strongly tied to low ACT and MCA scores, often regardless of background.
  • High academic readiness and PSEO participation predicted completions outside the region, fueling mobility pathways.

Implication: Regional institutions disproportionately serve disadvantaged students who achieve moderate readiness, while high scorers leave the region for completion.

2. Highest Credential Earned (highest.cred.level)

  • Bachelor’s or higher credentials were most strongly linked to high MCA and ACT scores, advantaged status, and PSEO participation.
  • Non-completion was almost guaranteed for students with low MCA Reading or ACT scores, even when other factors were favorable.
  • Associate or lower credentials (though less prominent in the tree structure) align with CTE-heavy pathways, serving as stabilizers for the regional workforce.

Implication: Academic readiness acts as the primary gateway to bachelor’s+ attainment, but economic and racial inequities remain barriers, even among high scorers.

Synthesis of Findings

Together, the models reinforce the existence of a dual-pipeline system in Southern Minnesota:

  1. Regional Workforce Pipeline
    • Driven by CTE engagement, associate-or-lower credentials, and regional completions.
    • Anchors students with economic disadvantage and moderate readiness into the local labor market.
    • Provides workforce stability but limits long-term mobility and earning potential.
  2. Mobility Pipeline
    • Driven by academic rigor, bachelor’s+ attainment, PSEO participation, and out-of-region completions.
    • Attracts advantaged, high-scoring students, who are least likely to remain in the region after completion.
    • Fuels statewide and out-of-state labor markets more than the local workforce.

Policy Implications

  • Strengthen regional anchors: Expand academic and financial supports for disadvantaged, moderate scorers to ensure successful completions in Southern MN institutions.
  • Address inequities in bachelor’s+ attainment: Target resources for high-achieving but low-income and underrepresented students to reduce barriers to advanced credentials.
  • Leverage CTE strategically: Maintain CTE as a stabilizer but ensure stackable pathways to prevent students from being locked into sub-associate outcomes.
  • Engage high-achievers locally: Create incentives and career opportunities that connect PSEO participants and bachelor’s+ completers back to the region to mitigate brain drain.

Conclusion

The predictive analysis confirms that academic readiness, economic status, and experiential factors like PSEO and CTE are the primary levers shaping postsecondary pathways. These pathways, in turn, determine whether students are most likely to remain in the Southern MN workforce or pursue mobility.

Ultimately, the models illustrate a structural duality: disadvantaged students with modest preparation form the backbone of the regional workforce through 2-year and regional completions, while advantaged high scorers are systematically funneled into statewide mobility. Policy efforts must therefore balance supporting the regional anchor pipeline with developing mechanisms to retain or re-engage mobile students after higher attainment.
