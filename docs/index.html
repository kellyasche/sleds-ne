Pathways After High School – Northeast Minnesota

Pathways to Meaningful Regional Employment in Northeast Minnesota

Purpose of the Research

This research examined which factors most strongly influence whether individuals achieve meaningful employment within Northeast Minnesota’s region (EDR 3) after high school. Using both contingency table analysis (effect sizes, trends over 1, 5, and 10 years) and CART modeling, the goal was to identify which variables most reliably anchor individuals in the regional workforce versus those that push them toward statewide mobility, disconnection, or outmigration.

A special focus was also placed on non-college graduates, who represent a substantial share of the region’s young adults and are at higher risk of disconnection from the labor force. Understanding their pathways provides additional insights into how to build a stronger, more inclusive regional workforce.

The findings provide a roadmap for education and workforce policy, showing how demographics, high school context, accomplishments, and postsecondary experiences interact to shape local labor market attachment.

Contingency Table Insights

Demographics

Demographic factors were relatively modest predictors overall. Special Education status showed the strongest short-term influence (V = 0.258, Yr 1), while Race/Ethnicity maintained moderate effects over time. Most demographic effects faded by 10 years, suggesting that institutional and educational pathways, not demographics alone, determine long-run anchoring.

For non-college graduates specifically, demographics had a sharper impact. SpecialEd students were persistently less likely to secure meaningful regional work and more likely to face disconnection. Economic status shaped outcomes differently: lower-income students were more region-bound but faced higher risks of unstable work, while higher-income peers more often pursued statewide opportunities.

High School Characteristics

Local economic context strongly influenced early outcomes. Unemployment rates (F = 53.1, Yr 1) and wage levels shaped whether graduates found local work or disconnected. Rural graduates were somewhat more likely to remain in meaningful regional jobs, while peers in urban or mixed settings pursued statewide employment. By 10 years, these contextual effects weakened, giving way to individual educational accomplishments.

High School Accomplishments

Clear patterns emerged between academic and technical preparation:
- Academic indicators (ACT, MCA, PSEO, AP) predicted statewide mobility and non-regional pathways.
- CTE achievement and course-taking were the strongest predictors of regional meaningful employment, with MCA Math showing the largest single predictive power overall (F = 1344.5, Yr 1).

For non-college graduates, CTE engagement was the most reliable pathway into meaningful regional employment, persisting through 10 years. By contrast, higher academic scorers and PSEO participants were less likely to anchor regionally, instead moving into statewide or out-of-region opportunities.

Postsecondary Experiences

For the full population, postsecondary experiences were the most powerful predictors overall:
- Attendance and completion of postsecondary education separated stable from disconnected pathways.
- Credential level and location nuanced outcomes:
- Regional/2-year credentials anchored locally,
- 4-year/out-of-state credentials expanded mobility,
- Non-completers were most at risk of poor outcomes.

Non-college graduates, by definition, did not experience these pathways, making their employment outcomes far more dependent on high school accomplishments and local economic context.

CART Analysis Findings

CART models confirmed and sharpened the contingency table results.

Anchors of Regional Employment

  1. Regional Postsecondary Completion anchored the broader graduate population long-term.
  2. CTE Engagement mattered across both groups. Two or more CTE courses increased Year 1 anchoring, while concentrators/completers sustained regional employment through Year 10 — even for non-college grads.
  3. Moderate Academic Readiness (ACT participation, MCA proficiency) anchored students regionally when paired with CTE or regional postsecondary experiences.

Persistent Barriers

  • Failure to complete postsecondary was the strongest barrier overall, though irrelevant for non-college grads.
  • SpecialEd needs consistently predicted disconnection in both groups.
  • Weak academics blocked employability and increased risk of unstable or non-meaningful work.
  • Unfavorable local economies (low wages, high unemployment) amplified disconnection risks, especially for non-college grads.

Postsecondary Pathways: Credential vs. Location

For students who attended postsecondary:
- Graduation Location determined whether graduates stayed rooted in the region. Regional institutions consistently anchored graduates, particularly disadvantaged but academically strong students.
- Highest Credential Level determined how far opportunities extended. Sub-baccalaureate degrees aligned closely with regional jobs, while bachelor’s and higher opened broader mobility. Non-completers faced the weakest outcomes.

For non-college graduates, these dynamics were absent. Their pathways were instead shaped by CTE participation, high school academics, and local economic conditions. CTE provided the anchoring force that postsecondary completion otherwise plays.

Non-College Graduate Insights

Relationships Analysis

  • CTE engagement was the strongest and most consistent predictor of meaningful regional work.
  • Lower ACT/MCA scorers were more likely to enter regional jobs, while higher scorers pursued broader opportunities.
  • SpecialEd students faced persistent disadvantages, rarely securing regional work.
  • Economic status showed mixed effects: lower-income grads were more region-bound but also more likely to disconnect.
  • Local context mattered: rural settings and stronger county economies improved anchoring.

Predictive Analysis

  • Year 1 models provided the clearest separation; predictive power declined over time.
  • CTE engagement remained the most durable anchor.
  • SpecialEd status was the most consistent barrier to connection.
  • Academic strength (especially ACT participation and scores) acted as mobility markers, steering students toward statewide jobs.
  • Local wages/unemployment set the broader context, with stronger labor markets supporting regional employment.

Overall, non-college graduates anchored regionally through CTE and favorable local economies, while academically strong students were more mobile and SpecialEd students faced long-term risks of disconnection.

Deep Dive: Non-College Graduates and Persistent Workforce Disconnection

This section extends the pathway analysis by focusing specifically on non-college graduates and examining factors associated with persistent workforce disconnection versus sustained local attachment in the five years after exiting formal education. While earlier sections identify broad postsecondary pathways and key predictors of workforce outcomes, this analysis narrows the lens to better understand why some non-college graduates consistently connect to the local labor force while others do not.

Rather than treating disconnection as a single outcome, this analysis distinguishes between: - Persistent disconnection,
- Intermittent or marginal attachment, and
- Sustained meaningful employment within the region,

and evaluates how individual characteristics relate to these outcomes year by year.

Analytical Approach

For non-college graduates only, relationships were examined between individual characteristics and: 1. Persistence of workforce disconnection, and
2. Workforce status in each of the first five years after exiting formal education, using refined employment categories that distinguish local meaningful employment, local underutilization, and absence from the Minnesota labor market.

Variables were grouped into four domains: - Demographics - High School Characteristics - High School Academics - Postsecondary Exposure (attendance without completion)

Associations were assessed using contingency tables and ANOVA tests. Given the large sample size, emphasis is placed on pattern consistency and substantive magnitude, not statistical significance alone.

Summary of Key Relationship Findings

Demographics

Demographic characteristics—including race/ethnicity, economic disadvantage, and special education status—show consistent but modest relationships with workforce outcomes. Economic disadvantage and special education status are associated with a higher likelihood of persistent disconnection and lower rates of meaningful local employment, while race and ethnicity show uneven patterns across outcomes.

Importantly, demographic variables do not strongly distinguish between local underutilization and complete absence from the regional labor force. These factors appear to shape baseline risk but do not fully explain long-term workforce trajectories.

High School Characteristics

District rurality shows a weak and stable relationship with workforce outcomes over time. Differences by rural, mixed, or urban district classification are statistically detectable but substantively small, and they do not meaningfully explain persistent disconnection once educational experiences are considered.

Similarly, county-level labor market conditions—measured by average unemployment rates and average wages relative to the state—explain virtually none of the variation in workforce outcomes. Across all five years, less than one percent of variation in these measures is associated with workforce category, indicating that persistent disconnection among non-college graduates is not driven by broad local economic conditions.

High School Academics

High school academic experiences show the clearest and most consistent relationships with workforce outcomes among non-college graduates.

  • CTE engagement, particularly CTE concentration/completion and higher numbers of CTE courses taken, is consistently associated with higher rates of meaningful local employment and lower rates of persistent disconnection.
  • Postsecondary exposure in high school (e.g., PSEO participation) is associated with improved workforce attachment, even among individuals who ultimately do not complete a credential.
  • ACT participation shows a strong and stable relationship with workforce outcomes, distinguishing individuals with sustained local attachment from those persistently disconnected.
  • Standardized test scores (ACT composite and MCA math, reading, and science) are related to workforce outcomes, but effect sizes are small. Higher scores are associated with better outcomes on average, yet score differences alone do not sharply differentiate persistent disconnection from intermittent or marginal attachment.

Overall, these findings suggest that applied learning experiences and exposure to postsecondary or career-oriented coursework matter more than test performance alone for long-term workforce connection among non-college graduates.

Postsecondary Exposure (Attendance Without Completion)

Postsecondary attendance—despite no credential completion—emerges as the single strongest and most consistent correlate of workforce outcomes in this analysis.

Across all five years after exit, individuals who attended postsecondary education are: - Substantially more likely to achieve meaningful employment within the region, and - Significantly less likely to have no Minnesota employment record.

The magnitude of these differences is large and stable over time, distinguishing postsecondary exposure from all other variables examined. While this relationship is correlational, it strongly suggests that even limited engagement with postsecondary education is associated with durable workforce attachment for non-college graduates.

Interpretation and Implications

Taken together, this deep dive reinforces several key conclusions:

  1. Persistent workforce disconnection among non-college graduates is not primarily a local labor market problem. County unemployment rates, wage levels, and district rurality explain little about who remains disconnected over time.
  2. Educational experiences—particularly applied and career-oriented ones—are central to long-term workforce outcomes. CTE participation, ACT-taking, and postsecondary exposure consistently differentiate sustained local attachment from persistent disconnection.
  3. Test scores matter less than exposure and engagement. While academic performance is related to outcomes, it does not by itself explain long-term workforce trajectories.
  4. Postsecondary exposure functions as a powerful signal and sorting mechanism, even without credential completion, potentially reflecting access to networks, information, expectations, or work-relevant skills.

This analysis complements the broader pathway and CART findings by clarifying which factors continue to matter after removing college completion from the equation. It highlights that for non-college graduates, early educational experiences and exposure—not local job availability—play the dominant role in shaping long-term workforce connection.

Synthesis and Policy Implications

Across all analyses, the findings support a dual-pathway model shaping workforce outcomes in Northeast Minnesota. These pathways operate for both college-goers and non-college graduates, though the mechanisms differ in important ways.

Anchoring Pathway: Career-Oriented Education and Regional Ties

The anchoring pathway is driven by career-aligned educational experiences and regional attachment.

For individuals who complete postsecondary education, regional enrollment and completion are strongly associated with sustained meaningful employment in the region. This pattern is consistent across years and workforce definitions, reinforcing the importance of place-based postsecondary pipelines.

For non-college graduates, a parallel anchoring mechanism emerges. CTE engagement—particularly reaching concentrator or completer status—and exposure to postsecondary education (even without completion) substantially increase the likelihood of meaningful regional employment and reduce long-term disconnection. These effects persist across all five years after exiting formal education.

Taken together, the evidence indicates that anchoring operates not only through credentials, but through structured exposure to applied learning environments, occupational signaling, and connections to employers. These mechanisms appear especially important for individuals with moderate academic preparation, for whom applied pathways translate education into labor force attachment more reliably than academic performance alone.

Mobility Pathway: Academic Preparation and Broader Opportunity

A second pathway reflects strong academic preparation combined with four-year or out-of-region postsecondary trajectories.

This pathway expands access to statewide and national labor markets and is often associated with outmigration. While it reduces the likelihood of regional employment, it yields higher credentials and broader opportunity. The persistence of this pattern suggests that mobility is not a failure of the regional system, but a predictable outcome for academically strong students.

The challenge for the region is therefore not to eliminate mobility, but to balance anchoring and mobility, ensuring that local opportunities are available and visible for those inclined to remain while supporting outward movement where it aligns with individual goals.

Clarifying the Drivers of Disconnection

Across demographic, academic, and educational variables, one finding is consistent: persistent workforce disconnection is weakly related to local economic conditions. County unemployment rates, wage levels, and district rurality explain little variation in long-term outcomes.

Instead, educational participation and structure dominate place-based factors. CTE engagement, ACT-taking, postsecondary attendance, and applied coursework consistently differentiate sustained attachment from persistent disconnection. Academic performance matters, but its effects are modest compared to participation and exposure.

Special Education status and economic disadvantage elevate baseline risk, but they do not determine outcomes. Individuals in these groups are more likely to disconnect in the absence of structured pathways, yet show substantially better outcomes when engaged in CTE or postsecondary environments.

Policy Implications

The findings point toward education-centered intervention levers, rather than labor-market-only solutions:

  • Strengthen and deepen CTE pathways, emphasizing progression to concentrator and completer status.
  • Expand structured postsecondary exposure, including dual enrollment, short-term credentials, and bridge programs, even when completion is uncertain.
  • Encourage applied academic engagement (e.g., ACT-taking, career-aligned coursework) as gateways to workforce attachment.
  • Provide targeted transition supports for Special Education students and academically weaker graduates, focusing on sustained engagement rather than remediation alone.
  • Align education-to-work transitions locally, ensuring that CTE and postsecondary experiences connect clearly to regional employers and occupations.

Efforts focused solely on increasing wages or reducing unemployment are unlikely to meaningfully reduce persistent disconnection without parallel investments in educational structure and exposure.

Conclusion

Meaningful workforce participation in Northeast Minnesota is shaped less by local labor market conditions and more by how individuals engage with education before and immediately after high school.

For college-goers, regional postsecondary completion remains a central anchoring mechanism. For non-college graduates, CTE engagement and postsecondary exposure play an equivalent role, substituting for the anchoring effect of degree completion.

At the same time, the mobility pathway remains important. Academically strong students will continue to pursue broader opportunities, and the region benefits when this mobility is intentional rather than driven by disconnection.

The most reliable strategies for strengthening the regional workforce are therefore complementary rather than singular:

  • Ensure deep engagement in CTE and applied learning,
  • Expand postsecondary exposure and on-ramps, even without guaranteed completion, and
  • Provide intentional supports for individuals at elevated risk of disconnection, particularly Special Education students and those with weaker academic preparation.

Sustaining a resilient regional workforce requires building multiple, clearly structured pathways to attachment, recognizing that for many young adults—especially non-college graduates—participation and exposure matter more than credentials alone.

Predictive employment CART Analysis

Employment Outcomes

This analysis examined what factors anchor young adults into meaningful employment in their high school region versus drifting into three alternative paths:
1. Employment elsewhere in Minnesota
2. Not meaningful employment/not attending postsecondary
3. No Minnesota employment record/not attending postsecondary

Using CART models at 1, 5, and 10 years after high school, the goal was to identify the most influential variables and extract actionable insights for regional workforce and education policy.

Key Anchors of Regional Employment

Across all models, three consistent anchors emerged:

  1. Regional Postsecondary Completion
    • By 5 and 10 years, the strongest predictor of staying anchored in the region is graduating from a postsecondary institution within the region.
    • Higher credential levels (associate and bachelor’s degrees) amplify this effect.
  2. CTE Engagement
    • In high school, simply taking 2+ CTE courses increases the likelihood of regional employment (especially by Year 1).
    • By Year 10, CTE concentrators and completers show significantly higher rates of remaining in the region, even when academic scores are modest.
  3. Moderate-to-Strong Academic Readiness
    • ACT participation and moderate ACT scores (not necessarily the highest) anchor students in the region early on.
    • MCA Math and Reading proficiency sustain attachment at later stages, providing a foundation for both CTE and postsecondary success.

Persistent Barriers

  1. Failure to Complete Postsecondary
    • Students who do not graduate from any postsecondary program are consistently at higher risk of disconnection or leaving the region.
  2. Weak Academic Preparation
    • Very low ACT scores and MCA performance are early barriers, limiting postsecondary access and undermining employability.
  3. Limited CTE Engagement
    • Students without depth in CTE (no concentration or completion) are more likely to fall into non-meaningful employment or lack a Minnesota employment record.
  4. Economic and Demographic Context
    • Low local wages and higher unemployment rates increase the risk of disconnection.
    • White students with weak academic or CTE preparation were more likely to leave or disconnect, while non-White students paired with CTE/PS pathways showed stronger anchoring.
    • Special Education status appears as a short-term barrier in Year 1, but its influence diminishes once postsecondary pathways are engaged.

Policy Implications for Northeast Minnesota

The modeling results point to clear levers for strengthening regional workforce attachment:

  1. Expand Regional Postsecondary Pipelines
    • Strengthen partnerships between high schools and regional colleges/universities.
    • Provide targeted supports to ensure not just access but completion of credentials in the region.
  2. Deepen CTE Pathways
    • Encourage students to progress from introductory courses to CTE concentrator/completer status, which has strong long-term anchoring effects.
    • Align CTE pathways tightly with regional industries and labor market needs.
  3. Support Academic Readiness at Moderate Levels
    • Ensure broad ACT participation and boost MCA Math/Reading performance.
    • Focus less on elite performance and more on raising the floor to “workforce-ready” levels.
  4. Mitigate Barriers for At-Risk Groups
    • Target interventions for students with low academic scores, no early CTE exposure, or no clear postsecondary plan.
    • Provide wage incentives and local internships to keep academically mobile students from leaving the region.

Conclusion

The results show that anchoring students in Northeast Minnesota’s workforce is not about producing the highest scorers or sending students to selective institutions elsewhere. Instead, the most powerful strategies are:

  • Ensuring students complete postsecondary programs within the region,
  • Building strong career and technical pathways, and
  • Raising academic preparation to moderate workforce-ready levels.

Barriers remain significant for those who leave postsecondary incomplete, underperform academically, or fail to engage in CTE. Addressing these gaps offers the most direct path to strengthening regional workforce resilience.


Relationships Post-secondary Pathways

Comparison of Postsecondary Graduation Location and Highest Credential Earned

Both postsecondary graduation location and highest credential earned emerged as powerful predictors of meaningful employment, but they influenced outcomes in different ways.

Graduation location primarily reflected where students were most likely to remain employed. The CART analysis showed that students who graduated from a regional institution were the most likely to secure meaningful local employment, demonstrating the strong anchoring effect of nearby colleges. By contrast, students graduating outside the region, especially outside Minnesota, were less likely to stay in the regional labor market. Academic readiness, particularly higher ACT scores, increased the likelihood of completing a regional credential, with disadvantaged students who scored well on the ACT especially anchored to local institutions.

Highest credential earned, on the other hand, shaped how far students’ employment opportunities extended. Students who attained a bachelor’s degree or higher were less likely to remain employed locally, as these credentials opened access to broader state and national labor markets. In contrast, students with associate degrees or lower were more likely to stay in regional employment, reflecting a closer alignment between sub-baccalaureate training and local job structures. Non-completers were at the highest risk of poor employment outcomes altogether, with many remaining in the region but in lower-skill, less stable jobs.

Summary and Policy Implications

Together, the two models highlight a complementary dynamic: graduation location determines whether students stay rooted in the region, while credential level determines whether they pursue local opportunities or broader labor markets. Regional institutions play a critical role in anchoring students, particularly those from disadvantaged backgrounds with strong academic preparation, while credential level shapes the degree of geographic mobility.

For policy and planning, this suggests two priorities. First, strengthening regional postsecondary institutions can enhance local workforce retention, especially by supporting high-achieving but economically constrained students who might otherwise leave. Second, expanding opportunities for sub-baccalaureate credentials can help align education with regional labor market needs, while creating clearer pathways for bachelor’s graduates to find meaningful employment locally may help reduce outmigration. In short, location ties students to place, while credential level influences the reach of their opportunities—a balance that policymakers and institutions can shape through program design and workforce partnerships.


Predictive post-secondary pathways CART

Overall Model Performance

Across both dependent variables — postsecondary graduation location and highest credential earned — the pre-pruned CART models achieved accuracies of 61.7% and 64.2%, respectively. Both represented clear improvements over baseline distributions and showed that a relatively small set of predictors consistently explains meaningful variation in student pathways.

While not capturing all individual-level complexity, these models highlight distinct sets of academic, demographic, and experiential variables that shape whether students remain anchored in the Southern MN workforce pipeline or pursue mobility pathways.

Key Predictors Across Models

The following predictors emerged as the most consistently influential:

  • Academic readiness: MCA Reading and ACT Composite Score.
  • Economic status: Persistent disadvantages for low-income students.
  • Race/Ethnicity: White and Asian/PI students more likely to reach higher credentials; Black, AI, and Hispanic students more often concentrated in lower or incomplete outcomes.
  • PSEO participation: Steers students toward bachelor’s+ and out-of-region completions.
  • CTE engagement (courses taken): Reinforces regional and sub-baccalaureate pathways.

These predictors operate in two clear pipelines — one driving local attachment through sub-baccalaureate credentials, and one fostering mobility through advanced credentials.

Interpretation of Pathways

1. Graduation Location (ps.grad.location)

  • Regional completions clustered among economically disadvantaged students with moderate academic readiness.
  • Non-completion was strongly tied to low ACT and MCA scores, often regardless of background.
  • High academic readiness and PSEO participation predicted completions outside the region, fueling mobility pathways.

Implication: Regional institutions disproportionately serve disadvantaged students who achieve moderate readiness, while high scorers leave the region for completion.

2. Highest Credential Earned (highest.cred.level)

  • Bachelor’s or higher credentials were most strongly linked to high MCA and ACT scores, advantaged status, and PSEO participation.
  • Non-completion was almost guaranteed for students with low MCA Reading or ACT scores, even when other factors were favorable.
  • Associate or lower credentials (though less prominent in the tree structure) align with CTE-heavy pathways, serving as stabilizers for the regional workforce.

Implication: Academic readiness acts as the primary gateway to bachelor’s+ attainment, but economic and racial inequities remain barriers, even among high scorers.

Synthesis of Findings

Together, the models reinforce the existence of a dual-pipeline system in Southern Minnesota:

  1. Regional Workforce Pipeline
    • Driven by CTE engagement, associate-or-lower credentials, and regional completions.
    • Anchors students with economic disadvantage and moderate readiness into the local labor market.
    • Provides workforce stability but limits long-term mobility and earning potential.
  2. Mobility Pipeline
    • Driven by academic rigor, bachelor’s+ attainment, PSEO participation, and out-of-region completions.
    • Attracts advantaged, high-scoring students, who are least likely to remain in the region after completion.
    • Fuels statewide and out-of-state labor markets more than the local workforce.

Policy Implications

  • Strengthen regional anchors: Expand academic and financial supports for disadvantaged, moderate scorers to ensure successful completions in Southern MN institutions.
  • Address inequities in bachelor’s+ attainment: Target resources for high-achieving but low-income and underrepresented students to reduce barriers to advanced credentials.
  • Leverage CTE strategically: Maintain CTE as a stabilizer but ensure stackable pathways to prevent students from being locked into sub-associate outcomes.
  • Engage high-achievers locally: Create incentives and career opportunities that connect PSEO participants and bachelor’s+ completers back to the region to mitigate brain drain.

Conclusion

The predictive analysis confirms that academic readiness, economic status, and experiential factors like PSEO and CTE are the primary levers shaping postsecondary pathways. These pathways, in turn, determine whether students are most likely to remain in the Southern MN workforce or pursue mobility.

Ultimately, the models illustrate a structural duality: disadvantaged students with modest preparation form the backbone of the regional workforce through 2-year and regional completions, while advantaged high scorers are systematically funneled into statewide mobility. Policy efforts must therefore balance supporting the regional anchor pipeline with developing mechanisms to retain or re-engage mobile students after higher attainment.

Deeper dive into non-college grads attachment

Overall Relationships Between Independent Variable Groups and Workforce Attachment

Across all analyses, clear patterns emerge in how different groups of variables relate to both persistent workforce disconnection and year-by-year workforce outcomes (after.ed.1 through after.ed.5) among non-college graduates in Northeast Minnesota.

Demographic variables show statistically significant but generally weak relationships with workforce outcomes. Economic disadvantage and Special Education status are associated with higher rates of disconnection, but effect sizes are modest, and these characteristics do not deterministically predict long-term outcomes. Gender differences are small and inconsistent across years.

High school characteristics, particularly those related to participation and engagement, show stronger and more stable relationships with workforce attachment. Variables such as ACT-taking, PSEO participation, and CTE achievement consistently differentiate individuals who remain attached to the labor force from those who become persistently disconnected. These relationships are evident immediately after exiting formal education and persist through five years out.

High school academic performance variables (ACT Composite Score and MCA Math, Reading, and Science) show statistically significant but relatively weak associations with workforce outcomes. Higher academic performance is associated with modestly better outcomes, particularly higher rates of meaningful employment, but these variables explain far less variation than participation-based measures. Academic performance alone does not reliably distinguish between sustained attachment and long-term disconnection.

Postsecondary participation (attended.ps) exhibits the strongest and most consistent relationship with workforce outcomes among non-college graduates. Individuals who attended postsecondary education—even without completion—are substantially more likely to achieve meaningful employment in the region and substantially less likely to have no Minnesota employment record across all five years after exit.

Importantly, these patterns are stable across time. Relationships observed in the first year after exit largely persist through year five, indicating that early post-exit trajectories tend to solidify rather than converge.

Key Insights on Drivers of Workforce Attachment and Disconnection

Several insights stand out when comparing the relative importance of variables:

  1. Participation matters more than performance.
    Engagement in structured educational experiences—CTE, postsecondary attendance, ACT-taking—consistently shows stronger relationships with workforce attachment than academic test scores.

  2. CTE plays a central anchoring role for non-college graduates.
    Individuals who are CTE concentrators or completers are more likely to maintain meaningful employment in the region and less likely to experience persistent disconnection. This pattern strengthens over time and mirrors the anchoring effect that postsecondary completion plays for college-goers.

  3. Postsecondary exposure without completion still matters.
    Attending postsecondary education, even without earning a credential, is associated with large differences in workforce outcomes. This suggests that exposure, signaling, and network effects may be as important as credentials themselves for labor force attachment.

  4. Academic achievement is a secondary, not primary, driver.
    While higher ACT and MCA scores are associated with better outcomes, the magnitude of these relationships is small compared to participation-based variables. Academic strength alone does not protect against disconnection in the absence of structured pathways.

  5. Persistent disconnection is not primarily a place-based economic problem.
    Local unemployment rates and wage conditions explain little of the observed variation. Disconnection appears driven more by educational pathways and transitions than by general labor market conditions.

Policy Implications

The findings point toward education-centered intervention strategies, particularly those focused on participation, structure, and transitions:

  • Expand and deepen CTE pathways, with emphasis on progression to concentrator and completer status rather than introductory exposure alone.
  • Increase structured postsecondary engagement, including dual enrollment, short-term credentials, and bridge programs, even when degree completion is uncertain.
  • Encourage applied academic engagement, such as ACT-taking and career-aligned coursework, as gateways to workforce attachment rather than purely academic sorting mechanisms.
  • Provide targeted transition supports for Special Education students and academically weaker graduates, focusing on sustained engagement with education or training rather than remediation alone.
  • Strengthen education-to-work alignment, ensuring that CTE and postsecondary experiences are clearly connected to regional employers and occupations.

Policies aimed solely at improving local labor market conditions are unlikely to substantially reduce persistent disconnection without parallel investments in educational participation and pathway structure.

Conclusion

Among non-college graduates in Northeast Minnesota, long-term workforce attachment is shaped far more by educational participation and pathway structure than by demographic characteristics, academic test performance, or local economic conditions.

CTE engagement and postsecondary attendance emerge as the most important differentiators between individuals who remain persistently connected to the labor force and those who experience sustained disconnection. These factors exert influence early and continue to shape outcomes through at least five years after exiting formal education.

Academic performance contributes modestly, but it does not substitute for structured engagement. Likewise, demographic risk factors elevate vulnerability but do not determine outcomes in the presence of strong educational pathways.

Overall, the evidence suggests that reducing persistent workforce disconnection among non-college graduates requires early, intentional, and sustained engagement in applied educational experiences, rather than relying on academic performance or labor market conditions alone.
