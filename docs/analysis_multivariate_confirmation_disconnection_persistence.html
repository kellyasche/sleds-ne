Multivariate Confirmation Analysis: Disconnection Persistence Among Non–Postsecondary Graduates

Introduction

The bivariate analysis of workforce disconnection among non-college graduates identified two distinct disconnection mechanisms operating within a single combined measure. Non-sustained employment (working, but below the sustained-employment threshold) and absence of any Minnesota employment record behave as structurally different phenomena: the former is front-loaded and largely resolves within five years, while the latter is bimodal and behaves as a durable end-state once established. Separating these two mechanisms revealed that different demographic and high-school-accomplishment variables predict each one — most notably, Special Education status and English learner status appeared to drive no-record persistence specifically, while Economic status and Gender appeared to drive non-sustained persistence specifically, and different racial/ethnic groups showed disparities concentrated in one mechanism or the other.

This multivariate analysis exists to confirm whether that mechanism-split pattern holds once all variables are considered simultaneously, rather than one at a time. This step is necessary because earlier work in this project has shown that bivariate relationships — particularly those involving English learner status — can substantially attenuate or shift once other variables, especially missing-data patterns in academic assessment scores, are accounted for in the same model. Before this mechanism-split finding is presented as a confirmed result, it should be tested under the same conditions.

Methodology

Study Population

The analytic sample is restricted to individuals who did not complete a postsecondary credential, consistent with the non-college-graduate population used throughout this project. The sample is further restricted to individuals who graduated (or exited postsecondary attendance, for those who attended without completing) after 2010, consistent with the cohort restriction applied elsewhere in this project to avoid the PSEO participation tracking gap present in earlier cohorts.

Dependent Variables

This analysis models two outcomes, each derived from the disconnection-persistence categories established in the bivariate analysis. Each graduate’s workforce status was tracked across the first five years after exiting formal education (high school for direct-to-workforce graduates, or postsecondary attendance for those who attended without completing).

  • Persistent non-sustained employment — binary (1 = non-sustained workforce participation in 4 or 5 of the first 5 years, 0 = otherwise). This isolates the instability mechanism identified in the bivariate work.
  • Persistent no-record status — binary (1 = no Minnesota employment record in 4 or 5 of the first 5 years, 0 = otherwise). This isolates the disappearance-from-the-system mechanism identified in the bivariate work.

Each outcome is modeled separately, in its own specification, rather than as levels of a single combined variable. This mirrors the logic of the bivariate analysis: combining the two mechanisms into one model would risk re-obscuring the distinction this analysis is specifically designed to confirm.

Independent Variables

The independent variable set is consistent with the variables used throughout this project’s non-college-graduate analyses, with two adjustments made based on findings from this project’s earlier work.

Demographics

  • Gender
  • RaceEthnicity
  • economic.status
  • SpecialEdStatus
  • english.learner

High school characteristics

  • Dem_Desc
  • avg.wages.pct.state
  • avg.unemp.rate

High school accomplishments

  • pseo.participant
  • ap.exam
  • took.ACT
  • ACTCompositeScore (recoded to 0 for non-test-takers, entered alongside took.ACT, per the missing-indicator approach established earlier in this project)
  • cte.achievement

Postsecondary variable

  • attended.ps — included specifically because it was the single strongest predictor of overall disconnection in the bivariate analysis, and it is the one variable found to meaningfully affect both disconnection mechanisms rather than only one.

Adjustments from the bivariate variable list:

  • MCA tests are excluded. There is enourmous amount of missing values and strangeness in the data so we are leaving them out entirely.
  • total.cte.courses.taken is excluded, consistent with the redundancy this project has established between the continuous course-count measure and the categorical cte.achievement variable.

Modeling Approach

Each outcome (persistent non-sustained status, persistent no-record status) is modeled using a staged block-entry sequence, consistent with the modeling structure used throughout this project’s other multivariate confirmation analyses. Predictors are introduced in three stages to make attenuation of any individual variable’s effect visible and attributable to a specific block, rather than only observable in a single final specification.

Model 1: Demographics and High School Characteristics

  • Demographics: Gender, RaceEthnicity, economic.status, SpecialEdStatus, english.learner
  • High school characteristics: Dem_Desc, avg.wages.pct.state, avg.unemp.rate

This baseline isolates whether structural and demographic positioning alone predicts persistence in either disconnection mechanism, before any measure of academic engagement or preparation is introduced.

Model 2: Add High School Accomplishments

  • Adds: pseo.participant, took.ACT, ACTCompositeScore.filled, ap.exam, cte.achievement

This is the model of primary interest for this analysis. Because English learner status has previously been shown to correlate with MCA Math missingness, this block specifically tests whether the English learner effect identified in Model 1 attenuates once high school accomplishments — including the MCA missingness indicator — are introduced. The same logic applies to Special Education status and any other Model 1 effect that might operate through academic engagement rather than independently.

Model 3: Add Postsecondary Attempt

  • Adds: attended.ps

This isolates whether attempting postsecondary education, identified in the bivariate analysis as the one variable meaningfully affecting both disconnection mechanisms, adds independent explanatory power once demographics and high school accomplishments are already accounted for, or whether its bivariate strength was substantially channeled through those same variables (e.g., PSEO participation, CTE achievement, or ACT engagement).

Each model is compared to the prior one using a likelihood ratio test to confirm it adds significant explanatory power, with AIC tracked across the sequence to summarize overall model improvement. This staged structure is applied identically to both outcomes, allowing direct comparison of how each mechanism’s predictors evolve across the same three blocks.

Evaluation Criteria

Results will be reported using odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and statistical significance levels. Interpretation will focus specifically on whether the demographic variables identified as mechanism-specific in the bivariate analysis (Special Education and English learner status for no-record persistence; Economic status and Gender for non-sustained persistence; racial/ethnic groups for whichever mechanism their bivariate disparity was concentrated in) remain significant, attenuate, or reverse once the full variable set is included. Findings will be described as associations holding other included variables constant. No causal claims will be made.


Prepping the data

The modeling dataset begins from the merged file containing both the disconnection-persistence categories (established in the bivariate analysis) and the full set of demographic, high school, and postsecondary variables used throughout this project. Three preparation steps are applied before modeling.

Cohort restriction. The sample is restricted to graduates from 2011 onward (grad.year > 2010). This mirrors the cohort restriction applied throughout this project’s other analyses of non-college graduates: PSEO participation was not reliably tracked prior to 2011 (missingness runs 92-100% in the 2008-2010 cohorts, dropping to near-zero by 2014), and including those cohorts would risk misclassifying untracked PSEO participants as confirmed non-participants.

Reference level assignment. Categorical variables are releveled to match the reference categories used consistently throughout this project (male, White, non-economically-disadvantaged, non-Special-Education, non-English-learner, entirely rural district, no CTE involvement, non-participant/non-test-taker for the binary accomplishment variables). This keeps odds ratios in this analysis directly comparable in direction and interpretation to every other model built in this project.

Missing-indicator recodes for two variables with structural or correlated missingness:

  • ACT composite score. Students who did not take the ACT have no composite score by definition — confirmed here to be a fully deterministic relationship with took.ACT (zero mismatches between the two). ACTCompositeScore is recoded to 0 for non-test-takers and entered into the model alongside took.ACT, so that took.ACT absorbs the effect of test participation itself while the filled score captures the achievement gradient among test-takers only.

Dependent variable construction. The two outcomes tested in this analysis are built directly from the bivariate persistence categories, collapsed to binary indicators:

  • persistent.non_sustained — 1 if the graduate was non-sustained in 4 or 5 of their first 5 years post-education-exit, 0 otherwise.
  • persistent.no_record — 1 if the graduate had no Minnesota employment record in 4 or 5 of those years, 0 otherwise.

Before fitting any models, missingness is checked across the full variable set to confirm the cohort restriction and missing-indicator recodes fully resolved known data-quality issues, and the English learner / MCA Math-missingness correlation is re-verified on this specific cohort-restricted sample to confirm the relationship under examination is actually present before testing whether the multivariate model absorbs it.

Model 1: Demographics and High School Characteristics

This model estimates the association between demographic characteristics and district context with persistent non-sustained employment (4-5 of the first 5 years post-education-exit), before any measure of academic engagement or preparation is introduced (n = 10,310).

This model does not fully replicate the bivariate pattern, and that discrepancy is worth flagging directly rather than smoothing over.

Gender behaves as expected — strong and significant. Female graduates show approximately 34% higher odds of persistent non-sustained employment relative to male graduates (OR ≈ 1.34, CI: 1.204-1.490, p < .001), consistent with Gender being one of the two strongest bivariate predictors of instability.

Economic status, by contrast, is not significant here (OR ≈ 1.07, CI: 0.956-1.195, p = .243) — despite being the other strongest bivariate predictor of non-sustained persistence. This is a real departure from the bivariate finding, not a rounding difference. One plausible explanation: economic disadvantage’s bivariate relationship with instability may be substantially confounded with race and disability status, both of which are now controlled for in this same model — but this is a hypothesis to note, not a conclusion to assert without further testing.

Special Education status is significant here (OR ≈ 1.36, CI: 1.189-1.562, p < .001) — the opposite of what the bivariate analysis suggested, where Special Ed was one of the weakest predictors of instability specifically (its effect was concentrated almost entirely in no-record persistence, not non-sustained persistence). This reversal is worth watching closely as later blocks are added, since it may indicate a suppression effect — Special Ed’s true relationship with instability may have been masked in the bivariate comparison by a confounding variable not yet identified.

Race/Ethnicity shows significant effects for three of four groups, generally consistent with or stronger than the bivariate pattern. American Indian graduates show higher odds (OR ≈ 1.37, consistent with the bivariate finding that AI graduates’ disadvantage is instability-driven). Black graduates show the largest race-based effect in this model (OR ≈ 1.68), also consistent with the bivariate direction. Asian/Pacific Islander graduates show significantly lower odds (OR ≈ 0.56) — the same direction found bivariately (Asian/PI showed the lowest persistent-instability rate of any group). Hispanic graduates show no significant effect here (OR ≈ 1.04, CI crosses 1), consistent with the bivariate finding that this group’s disadvantage was concentrated in no-record status rather than instability.

District characteristics remain uniformly non-significant, exactly as the bivariate analysis found: Dem_Desc (both categories), average local wages, and average local unemployment rate all show confidence intervals crossing 1 with no meaningful effect size.

Overall Interpretation

Two of the five demographic predictors — Economic status and Special Education status — do not behave as the bivariate analysis would predict once controlled for alongside the others, and in opposite directions from each other. Gender and the racial disparities largely replicate the bivariate pattern. District/geographic context remains entirely non-predictive, consistent with every other model in this project’s disconnection work. The Economic status and Special Ed reversals are the two findings to track most closely once high school accomplishments are added in Model 2 — it’s possible one or both of these patterns shift again once academic engagement variables, which correlate with both economic status and disability status, are accounted for.

Model fit: AIC = 9,092.4; residual deviance = 9,066.4 on 10,297 df (null deviance = 9,162.9 on 10,309 df); n = 10,310.

Model 3: Add Postsecondary Attempt

This model adds attended.ps to the full specification (n = 10,128, same complete-case sample as Model 2).

Attempting postsecondary education shows a strong, independent protective effect on top of demographics, district context, and high school accomplishments — confirming this variable adds real explanatory power beyond what CTE, PSEO, and ACT already capture. OR ≈ 0.715 (CI: 0.632-0.808, p < .001): students who attempted postsecondary education, even without completing it, show roughly 29% lower odds of persistent non-sustained employment than those who never attempted it. This confirms the bivariate finding that attended.ps was the one variable affecting both disconnection mechanisms — its instability-side effect survives full multivariate control, not just partial control.

PSEO’s adverse effect from Model 2 persists essentially unchanged (OR ≈ 1.22, CI: 1.076-1.384, p = .002) — even with postsecondary attempt now in the model, PSEO participation continues to show a mild, real association with higher odds of persistent instability, not lower.

Took-ACT and ACT score both weaken further and no longer approach significance. Took-ACT moves from marginal in Model 2 (p = .069) to clearly non-significant here (OR ≈ 0.734, CI: 0.495-1.086, p = .123). ACT composite score remains only marginal (OR ≈ 1.018, p = .074, CI: 0.998-1.037). This suggests some of ACT’s apparent relationship with instability in Model 2 was capturing information that attended.ps explains more directly — sensible, since taking the ACT and attempting postsecondary education are closely related behaviors.

Special Education status remains essentially unchanged and still significant (OR ≈ 1.43, CI: 1.232-1.647, p < .001) — confirming again that this effect is not explained by postsecondary attempt any more than it was explained by high school accomplishments in Model 2. This is now the third model in a row where Special Ed’s adverse association with instability holds up.

Economic status and English learner status both remain non-significant (economic status: OR ≈ 1.11, p = .080; English learner: OR ≈ 0.95, p = .614), consistent with every prior model — neither shows any relationship with persistent instability once other variables are controlled for.

Summary: Multivariate Confirmation — Persistent Non-Sustained Employment

Across all three models, several bivariate findings held up, but one demographic effect reversed direction in a way worth treating as this analysis’s central discovery.

Special Education status is a genuine, positive predictor of instability — the opposite of what the bivariate analysis suggested. Bivariately, Special Ed appeared to be almost entirely a no-record phenomenon with little relationship to instability specifically. Once demographics, district context, high school accomplishments, and postsecondary attempt are all controlled for, Special Ed shows a strong, stable, significant association with higher odds of persistent non-sustained employment (OR ≈ 1.36–1.45 across all three models) that never attenuates, no matter what else is added to the model. This is a real reversal, not a bivariate artifact resolving itself — Special Ed’s relationship with instability appears to have been suppressed in the bivariate comparison, not masked by something the multivariate model then explains away.

Economic status and English learner status never emerge as predictors of instability at any stage, confirming the bivariate finding cleanly: whatever drives these two groups toward overall disconnection, it is not the instability mechanism.

PSEO participation behaves in a way that runs counter to its usual “broadly protective” role elsewhere in this project. It shows a small but real adverse association with instability (OR ≈ 1.21–1.22, consistent with the bivariate finding), holding steady across every model once other variables are controlled.

ACT participation and score both trend in the expected protective/adverse directions, but neither holds up as a reliable, independent predictor. Both sit at the edge of significance once high school accomplishments are added, and both fall further from significance once postsecondary attempt enters the model — suggesting whatever relationship they have with instability is substantially overlapping with attempting postsecondary education itself, rather than representing a distinct, independent effect.

Attempting postsecondary education is confirmed as an independent, genuine predictor of reduced instability, surviving full control alongside CTE, PSEO, and ACT (OR ≈ 0.72 in the final model) — this is the clearest bivariate finding to survive multivariate testing fully intact.

Race/Ethnicity effects for American Indian, Black, and Asian/Pacific Islander graduates all replicate their bivariate direction and remain significant across every model — American Indian and Black graduates show elevated odds of instability, Asian/Pacific Islander graduates show reduced odds, consistent with the bivariate finding that these groups’ disadvantage (or advantage) is genuinely instability-driven. Hispanic graduates show no significant effect at any stage, consistent with the bivariate finding that this group’s disadvantage is concentrated in no-record status rather than instability.

CTE achievement and AP exam, both real bivariate predictors of instability, do not survive multivariate control — neither reaches significance once entered alongside the other accomplishment variables, suggesting their bivariate relationships were substantially explained by other correlated variables now in the model.

Local geographic and economic context (Dem_Desc, wages, unemployment) remains entirely non-predictive across all three models, confirming this project’s consistent finding that local labor market conditions do not meaningfully shape workforce instability for this population.

Summary: Multivariate Confirmation — Persistent No Minnesota Employment Record

Across all three models, this analysis delivered a clear, stable central finding, along with several supporting results.

English learner status is a strong, persistent predictor of no-record status across every stage of control. OR moves from 1.94 (Model 1) to 1.79 (Model 2) to 1.71 (Model 3) — a modest, gradual decline, but English learners remain at nearly double the odds of persistent no-record status relative to non-English-learners even in the fully specified model (p < .001 throughout). This is the clearest and most consequential finding in this analysis: English learners’ disadvantage in disappearing from Minnesota’s employment records is not explained away by high school accomplishments or postsecondary attempt — it holds up as an independent, substantial effect.

The same pattern holds for Hispanic and Asian/Pacific Islander graduates. Both groups show large, significant effects in the demographics-only model (Hispanic OR ≈ 2.42; Asian/PI OR ≈ 2.41) that remain strong and significant through the full model sequence (Hispanic OR ≈ 2.29-2.32; Asian/PI OR ≈ 2.12-2.24, both p < .001 throughout). Black graduates also remain significant at every stage (OR ≈ 1.41-1.47, p < .01 throughout). American Indian graduates remain non-significant or marginal across all three models (OR ≈ 1.15-1.18), consistent with the bivariate finding that AI graduates’ disadvantage concentrates in the instability mechanism rather than this one.

Took-ACT is the single most powerful predictor found anywhere in this analysis. OR remains in the 0.26-0.30 range across both models where it appears — students who took the ACT show roughly 70-74% lower odds of persistent no-record status. ACT composite score confirms the expected threshold-effect pattern, with higher scores among test-takers predicting modestly higher odds of no-record status (OR ≈ 1.05 per point, p < .001 in both models).

Attempting postsecondary education is confirmed as a large, genuine, independent predictor of reduced no-record risk, holding up fully alongside every other variable (OR ≈ 0.53 in the final model) — a larger effect than it showed in the non-sustained-employment sequence, consistent with the bivariate finding that attended.ps’s protective effect concentrates more heavily in this mechanism.

PSEO participation and CTE achievement both confirm substantial, stable protective effects across every model in which they appear, consistent with the bivariate finding that these variables’ broad protective role for this population runs heavily through avoiding no-record status specifically.

Special Education status shows no relationship with no-record status at any stage — a clean, consistent null result across all three models, confirming that Special Ed’s disconnection story (established in the companion non-sustained-employment analysis) is an instability story, not a no-record story, despite the bivariate comparison initially suggesting otherwise.

Economic status and Gender both emerge as modest, genuine, stable protective predictors, holding up consistently across all three models. Average local unemployment rate is significant in Model 2 but weakens to marginal in Model 3 once postsecondary attempt is added — a real shift worth tracking rather than treating the Model 2 result as final. AP exam and district geographic classification remain non-significant throughout.

The Story This Comparison Tells

The core mechanism-split finding survives largely intact. The bivariate analysis suggested Special Education and English learner status were both primarily no-record phenomena. The multivariate models confirm this only partially: Special Education status turns out to be an instability phenomenon, not a no-record phenomenon at all — the opposite of what the bivariate comparison suggested. English learner status, by contrast, is confirmed as a genuine, robust no-record phenomenon — its effect declines only modestly across the three models and remains highly significant throughout.

Race/Ethnicity delivers the most important finding of this analysis: the Hispanic, Black, and Asian/Pacific Islander disparities in no-record status are real, independent, and do not attenuate. All three groups remain significantly elevated at every stage of control, including after high school accomplishments and postsecondary attempt are both accounted for.

Asian/Pacific Islander graduates show a striking divergence between the two mechanisms — significantly reduced odds of instability, but among the highest odds of no-record status of any group. This confirms that different racial/ethnic groups experience genuinely different, sometimes opposite, forms of disadvantage depending on which outcome is examined.

attended.ps, PSEO, and CTE achievement all confirm strong, stable roles in the no-record mechanism specifically — three of the clearest, most consistent findings in this analysis, with no attenuation or reversal at any stage.

What This Means for the Report

Three things are ready to go into the report as confirmed, multivariate-tested findings: (1) postsecondary attempt, PSEO participation, and CTE achievement’s mechanism-specific protective effects on no-record status; (2) English learner status and the Hispanic, Black, and Asian/Pacific Islander disparities in no-record status are robust, independent findings — they hold up fully once high school accomplishments and postsecondary attempt are controlled for, and should be presented as confirmed disparities, not as effects requiring further explanation; and (3) Special Education status’s correction from a no-record story to an instability story. Given that the central race/ethnicity and English learner findings replicate cleanly across bivariate and multivariate stages, the report can present these as a single, consistent story.
