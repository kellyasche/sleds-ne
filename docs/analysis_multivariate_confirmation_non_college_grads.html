Multivariate Confirmation Analysis: Non–Postsecondary Graduates

The primary purpose of this supplemental analysis is to examine workforce participation pathways among individuals who did not complete a postsecondary credential. Earlier sections focused on the full population and demonstrated that postsecondary attainment—particularly where and how a credential was earned—was the dominant mechanism associated with sustained regional employment.

This section shifts attention to the population for whom that mechanism does not operate.

Among non–postsecondary graduates, workforce participation pathways cannot be explained by credential depth, institutional sector, or graduation location. Instead, the bivariate analyses identified high school career alignment (CTE), English learner status, structural disadvantage, and local labor market context as central differentiators of workforce outcomes. English learner status in particular shows the single largest demographic effect size of any variable examined in this subgroup, and is included here as its own predictor rather than treated as a pattern explained by other variables.

To strengthen confidence in those findings, a limited multivariate analysis is conducted using logistic regression. As in the full-population analysis, the goal is not causal inference or prediction, but confirmatory testing. Specifically, this modeling examines whether the strongest bivariate relationships remain directionally consistent when key predictors are considered simultaneously.

Logistic regression is used here as a confirmatory tool rather than the primary analytic framework.


Methodology

Study Population

The analytic sample is restricted to individuals who did not complete a postsecondary credential. This restriction isolates workforce dynamics among high school graduates entering the labor market directly.

Outcome Variable

The primary model predicts:

  • Sustained employment – region (binary outcome)

The dependent variable is coded as:

  • 1 = Sustained employment – region
  • 0 = All other workforce outcomes (including sustained employment – MN outside the region, non-sustained employment, and no MN employment record)

This specification maintains consistency with earlier modeling while isolating mechanisms specific to the non-college population.

Independent Variables

Because postsecondary mechanisms are not applicable in this subgroup, the model focuses on high school preparation, structural background, and local labor market context identified as important in earlier analyses.

Predictors include:

  • Demographic
    • Gender
    • RaceEthnicity
    • economic.status
    • SpecialEdStatus
    • english.learner
  • High school and local context
    • Dem_Desc
    • range.school
    • avg.wages.pct.state
    • avg.unemp.rate
  • High school accomplishments
    • cte.achievement
    • took.ACT
    • ACTCompositeScore_filled
    • pseo.participant
    • ap.exam
    • sat.taken

Variables are selected based on:

  1. Strength of association in prior subgroup analyses
  2. Conceptual plausibility within a non-college pathway
  3. Policy relevance
  4. Avoidance of redundancy

Postsecondary variables are excluded by design.

Range School and SAT Participation

range.school and sat.taken were not included in the original bivariate analysis of this subgroup, and were added here following supplemental bivariate testing conducted specifically for this population.

Both variables show a real, statistically significant relationship with workforce participation outcomes among non-college graduates at all three time points (Year 1, 5, and 10). Range school status shows a consistent, substantively sensible pattern: Range graduates are more likely to land in county-level meaningful employment specifically, with no corresponding difference in disconnection rates — a precise, geography-specific signal distinct from the broader “no MN employment record” and “not meaningful” categories. SAT participation shows a smaller but real relationship, with SAT-takers in this subgroup showing a notably higher and growing rate of no Minnesota employment record over time; given the very small number of SAT-takers who don’t complete a postsecondary credential (n < 150 at every time point), this finding should be treated as suggestive rather than precise, but it clears the same bar for inclusion applied to every other variable in this project.

Both variables are therefore included in the multivariate specification on the same basis as every other predictor in this analysis: a demonstrated, non-trivial bivariate relationship with the outcome under study.


Analysis Approach

This multivariate analysis mirrors the structured confirmatory framework used in the full-population models but is adapted to the non-college subgroup.

The dependent variable is a binary indicator of Sustained employment – region.

Time-Based Modeling Strategy

Workforce attachment evolves over time. To capture this dynamic process, models will be estimated separately at:

  • 1 year after high school
  • 5 years after high school
  • 10 years after high school

This staged approach allows examination of how mechanisms change across early, mid-term, and longer-term workforce trajectories.

Year 1: Immediate Labor Market Entry

The 1-year model captures early transition into the labor market. At this stage, high school preparation and career alignment are expected to exert the strongest influence. CTE exposure and intensity may function as immediate workforce anchors, while structural disadvantage may strongly shape early labor market attachment.

Year 5: Stabilization Phase

The 5-year model reflects early career consolidation. At this point, initial preparation effects may attenuate, and differences associated with labor market context and structural disadvantage may become more pronounced. This model evaluates whether early alignment persists or whether structural conditions increasingly shape outcomes.

Year 10: Long-Term Embeddedness

The 10-year model captures longer-term workforce integration. Earlier analyses suggest that local labor market conditions (e.g., wages and unemployment) may become more dominant over time. This specification tests whether contextual economic factors increasingly explain sustained regional employment among non-college graduates as individual preparation effects diminish.

Estimating separate models at each time point allows for:

  • Evaluation of coefficient stability over time
  • Assessment of attenuation or amplification of predictors
  • Identification of shifting mechanisms from individual preparation to structural context

Modeling Strategy

Within each time period, predictors are introduced in a structured sequence:

Model 1: Demographics

Includes demographic variables:

  • Demographics
    • Gender
    • RaceEthnicity
    • economic.status
    • SpecialEdStatus

Model 2: Add high school characteristics

  • High school characteristics
    • Dem_Desc
    • range.school
    • avg.wages.pct.state
    • avg.unemp.rate

Model 3: Add high school accomplishments

  • High school accomplishments
    • cte.achievement
    • pseo.participant
    • took.ACT
    • ACTCompositeScore_filled
    • ap.exam
    • sat.taken
    • Evaluation Criteria

Results will be reported using:

  • Odds ratios
  • 95% confidence intervals
  • Statistical significance levels
  • Directional interpretation

Model comparison metrics (AIC and pseudo R²) will be used to assess relative improvement across specifications, but emphasis will remain on coefficient magnitude and stability across time.

Interpretation Framework

Interpretation will focus on:

  • Whether CTE retains independent influence across time
  • Whether structural disadvantage grows or attenuates
  • Whether labor market context becomes increasingly dominant
  • How mechanisms shift from Year 1 to Year 10

Findings will be described as associations holding other included variables constant. No causal claims will be made.


Conceptual Focus

For non–postsecondary graduates, this time-structured modeling framework evaluates three evolving mechanisms:

  1. Career alignment at labor market entry
  2. Structural advantage or disadvantage shaping early stability
  3. Local economic opportunity structures influencing long-term embeddedness

Together, these models clarify whether sustained regional employment among non-college graduates reflects early career alignment, structural inequality, changing economic context, or a combination of all three across time.

Summary: Multivariate Confirmation — Non-College Graduate Workforce Participation (Year 1, 5, and 10)

Across all three time points, this analysis identifies a small set of disparities that are genuinely durable — some of which widen rather than narrow over a decade — alongside several that are significant early on but become undetectable at Year 10, where the much smaller sample size makes it difficult to distinguish a resolved disparity from a statistical-power limitation.

Black graduates show the single most robust, and widening, disparity found anywhere in this three-model sequence. Significant and substantial at every stage of every time point, the gap actually grows over time: OR ≈ 0.52 at Year 1, 0.45 at Year 5, and 0.30 at Year 10 (fully adjusted models). Ten years out, Black non-college graduates show roughly a two-thirds reduction in odds of sustained regional employment relative to White graduates — the largest disparity identified in this entire analysis.

Gender shows a similarly durable and widening pattern. Significant at every stage of every time point, moving from OR ≈ 0.94 (Year 1, marginal by the final model) to 0.85 (Year 5) to 0.77 (Year 10) — the gap between male and female non-college graduates’ odds of sustained regional employment steadily widens across the full ten-year window.

Asian/Pacific Islander graduates show a significant disparity at both endpoints (Year 1 and Year 10), though it temporarily loses significance partway through the Year 5 sequence once academic accomplishments are added (OR ≈ 0.73, p = .106) — a real, if not perfectly consistent, disadvantage across the ten-year window.

English learner status and Special Education status both show strong, strengthening, independent disparities at Year 1 and Year 5, but are not detectable by Year 10. Both hold up robustly across all three models at the earlier two time points — if anything, strengthening once academic accomplishments are considered — but neither reaches significance at Year 10, where the sample is roughly a third the size of the earlier models. Given how consistently these effects held up earlier, this is better read as a power limitation than evidence the disparities resolve by Year 10.

Hispanic graduates show a significant disparity at Year 1 and Year 5 that is not detectable at Year 10, following the same pattern as English learner and Special Ed status, and likely for the same reason.

American Indian graduates show a distinct pattern: no relationship at Year 1, a brief, significant disadvantage at Year 5, and no relationship again at Year 10 — the only demographic group whose significance is confined specifically to the middle time point.

Economic status follows an inverted pattern: no relationship at Year 1, a clear, significant disadvantage at Year 5, and only a marginal relationship at Year 10 (p = .052) — consistent with disparities that take time to emerge after graduation, though the Year 10 result should be read cautiously given the smaller sample.

District classification shows a similar delayed-emergence pattern. No relationship at Year 1, a significant advantage for Urban/town-rural mix districts at Year 5, and only a marginal relationship at Year 10 — geography’s importance for this subgroup appears to build over the first five years before fading (or becoming undetectable) by Year 10.

Range school status, local wages, and local unemployment rate show essentially no relationship with regional employment for non-college graduates at any time point — a clear and consistent departure from these same variables’ strong, independent roles throughout the rest of this project’s full-population and completers-only models.

PSEO participation shows a real, positive, independent effect only at Year 1 (OR ≈ 1.13), and shows no relationship at Year 5 or Year 10 — its benefit for this specific subgroup appears to be front-loaded and short-lived rather than a lasting advantage.

The ACT crossover pattern replicates at all three time points, consistent with the rest of this project, though it weakens in magnitude by Year 10. Taking the ACT predicts higher odds of sustained regional employment at every time point (OR ≈ 2.94, 2.78, and 1.97 respectively), while among test-takers, higher composite scores predict lower odds at every time point as well — the same threshold effect found throughout this project’s other analyses.

AP exam participation shows a significant negative relationship at Year 1 and Year 5, but not at Year 10. The Story This Data Tells

Picture a student who graduates from high school in Northeast Minnesota without going on to complete a postsecondary credential. What predicts whether they’re sustainably employed in the region — not just next year, but a decade from now?

Two disparities stand out as genuinely durable, and both get worse, not better, with time. Black graduates and, to a lesser extent, women both start out somewhat disadvantaged relative to their peers in Year 1 — and by Year 10, both gaps have widened substantially. This isn’t a story of early struggles that resolve with time; for these two groups, the region sustains, rather than closes, its differences in workforce attachment over a full decade.

Several other disparities look real and meaningful in the first five years, then become impossible to detect by Year 10 — but that’s very likely a story about sample size, not about disparities disappearing. English learner status, Special Education status, and Hispanic identity all show strong, independent, even strengthening relationships with reduced regional employment at Year 1 and Year 5. By Year 10, the sample has shrunk to less than half its earlier size — simply because fewer graduates from earlier cohorts have had ten years pass yet — and none of these three effects clear the bar for statistical significance anymore. Given how consistently and robustly they held up in the two earlier, better-powered models, the responsible reading is “we can no longer detect this with confidence at this smaller sample size,” not “this disparity has resolved.”

A few disparities appear to take time to develop rather than being present from the start. Economic disadvantage and district geography both show no relationship in Year 1, then emerge as real, significant predictors by Year 5 — suggesting some disadvantages compound gradually after graduation rather than showing up immediately.

One story runs opposite to what’s found almost everywhere else in this project: local geography and economic conditions barely matter here. Range school status, local wages, and local unemployment rate — all strong, independent predictors of workforce and postsecondary outcomes for the full graduating population and for college completers — show essentially no relationship with regional employment for non-college graduates at any point in this ten-year window. For this specific population, it isn’t where you’re from or the local economy that matters most — it’s what you did in high school.

And that “what you did in high school” story has one clear headline: CTE. Career and technical education isn’t just a positive predictor here — it’s the single most consistent, strengthening effect in the entire analysis, growing more protective the further out you look. The familiar ACT pattern from elsewhere in this project shows up here too — simply taking the test predicts staying regionally employed, while a strong score predicts something else — but its strength fades somewhat by Year 10, while CTE’s grows. For non-college graduates specifically, high school career-and-technical pathways look like the most durable, long-term lever this project has identified for sustained regional workforce attachment.

PSEO tells a narrower story here than it does elsewhere in this project. It shows a real, independent benefit immediately after graduation, but that advantage doesn’t persist — by Year 5 and Year 10, PSEO participation shows no relationship with regional employment at all. For non-college graduates, PSEO looks like it helps with the transition out of high school, not with long-term regional attachment.

Put together, this paints a somewhat different picture of “who is at risk” than the full-population and completers analyses did. For graduates who don’t go on to any postsecondary credential, the most durable, decade-long risks are concentrated in race (specifically Black identity) and gender — both worsening over time — while several other demographic disparities are real in the near term but harder to track with confidence a decade out. And unlike almost every other outcome examined in this project, geography and the local economy take a back seat: what happens in high school — CTE engagement above all — matters more for this population’s long-term regional employment than where they’re from.
