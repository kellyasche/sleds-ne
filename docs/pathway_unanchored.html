Pathway - Unanchored / At-Risk

Pathway: Unanchored / At-Risk

Limited or unstable attachment to the workforce

Overview

This pathway describes high school graduates who do not go on to complete a postsecondary credential and who struggle to establish stable employment in the years that follow. Unlike graduates who find steady work — whether in the region or elsewhere in Minnesota — individuals on this pathway are more likely to spend years cycling through unstable work, or to disappear entirely from Minnesota’s tracked employment system.

That second sentence points to something important: “disconnected from the workforce” is not one single experience. It actually describes two different things that happen to look similar from a distance but behave very differently up close. This report walks through what that distinction means, how large this population is, and what the data says about who is most affected and why it matters for regional workforce planning.

How Common Is This Pathway?

Approximately 58% of high school graduates in this region do not complete a postsecondary credential. This pathway is concerned specifically with this group — not because failing to finish college is itself a problem, but because a substantial share of these graduates go on to experience prolonged difficulty finding steady work, while others do not. Understanding what separates the two is the point of this analysis.

Among these non-college graduates, tracked over their first five years after leaving formal education (high school for those who went straight to work, or the point they stopped attending college for those who tried it without finishing):

  • About 1 in 8 (roughly 12%) are consistently, steadily employed in every one of those five years.
  • The remaining majority experience some meaningful period of disconnection from stable work — often more than one year, and for many, more than half of the entire five-year window.

Stable, uninterrupted employment is the exception for this population, not the norm.

Two Different Kinds of Disconnection

Before going further, it’s worth being precise about what “disconnected from the workforce” actually means in this data, because it covers two genuinely different situations.

Unstable employment means someone has a record of working in Minnesota, but not steadily enough to count as meaningfully, sustainably employed — think part-time, seasonal, or frequently-interrupted work.

No Minnesota employment record means there is no evidence that person worked in Minnesota at all in a given year. This could mean they moved out of state for work, or it could mean they are genuinely disconnected from the labor force — the data alone can’t always distinguish between the two.

These aren’t just two labels for the same underlying problem — they behave like two different phenomena entirely:

  • Unstable employment tends to resolve over time. Most graduates who experience it don’t stay in that state for long: about 4 in 10 experience it only briefly (one or two years out of five), and it’s the least common outcome for someone to remain unstably employed persistently across nearly the entire five-year window (about 1 in 6).
  • Having no Minnesota employment record behaves very differently — it’s an all-or-nothing pattern. Just over half of non-college graduates never experience this at all. But among those who do, it’s disproportionately likely to become a lasting condition rather than a passing one: roughly 1 in 5 non-college graduates fall into this state persistently, for most or all of their first five years.

Put simply: unstable work is common but usually temporary. Disappearing from the employment record entirely is less common, but when it happens, it tends to stick.

This distinction matters because the two patterns point toward different underlying situations, and potentially different responses. A graduate cycling through unstable jobs is a job-quality and advancement problem. A graduate who disappears from the record for years at a time is a much harder question — possibly reflecting genuine disconnection from work altogether, out-of-state moves, or informal work the state’s records simply don’t capture.

Key Characteristics of the Unanchored / At-Risk Pathway

Lack of postsecondary completion. This is the single strongest and most consistent dividing line in this analysis. The roughly 58% of graduates who do not complete a postsecondary credential are disproportionately represented among those experiencing either form of disconnection described above. Attempting postsecondary education — even without finishing it — meaningfully lowers the odds of both unstable employment and disappearing from the employment record, and this holds up even after accounting for every other factor examined in this analysis.

Limited Career and Technical Education (CTE) engagement. Participation in CTE coursework in high school is one of the clearest protective factors identified in this analysis. Graduates who complete a CTE concentration are meaningfully more likely to reach sustained, stable employment than those with no CTE exposure, and this pattern holds consistently over time.

Academic engagement and preparation. Simply taking the ACT — regardless of the score received — is associated with meaningfully lower odds of disconnection, particularly of the no-record type, where ACT test-takers show roughly a two-thirds reduction in odds relative to non-test-takers. This holds even after controlling for every other factor examined. Academic proficiency itself tells a narrower story: lower math proficiency is associated with a higher likelihood of having no Minnesota employment record, but shows little independent relationship with unstable-but-present employment specifically.

Special Education status. Graduates who received special education services show a real, independent, and unusually persistent association with unstable employment specifically — not with disappearing from the record. This relationship holds up strongly across every version of the analysis, regardless of what other factors are accounted for, making it one of the most reliable findings in this entire pathway.

Workforce Outcomes Over Time

Tracked year by year after leaving formal education, a consistent pattern emerges: sustained employment (whether regional or elsewhere in Minnesota) grows steadily, rising from roughly 30% of this population in the first year to about 40% by the fifth year. Unstable employment shrinks over that same period, falling from about 43% to about 26% of the population.

But the share of graduates with no Minnesota employment record does not shrink — it grows, from roughly 26% in the first year to about 33% by year five, eventually becoming the single largest outcome of any kind for this population. In other words, as early instability resolves, it resolves mostly into either steady employment or disappearance from the record — not evenly, but with disappearance from the record growing the fastest of any single outcome.

County-level labor market conditions — such as local wages and unemployment rates — explain very little of this pattern. Graduates in stronger and weaker local economies show broadly similar rates of disconnection, suggesting that individual circumstances and preparation matter far more than the strength of the local economy alone.

Disparities Within the Unanchored / At-Risk Pathway

Workforce disconnection is not evenly distributed, and — consistent with the distinction drawn earlier — different groups tend to experience different kinds of disconnection, not just different overall rates.

Groups whose elevated risk shows up mainly as unstable employment:

  • Special Education status: graduates with special education services show a real, independent elevation in unstable employment specifically.
  • Economic disadvantage: graduates who qualified for free or reduced-price lunch are more likely to experience persistent instability (roughly 17% vs. 14% for their peers) and less likely to avoid instability altogether.
  • Female graduates show a similar pattern to economic disadvantage — modestly higher odds of persistent instability than male graduates (roughly 18% vs. 14%).
  • American Indian graduates show an elevated risk of unstable employment specifically, a pattern that holds up independently of other factors examined.

Groups whose elevated risk shows up mainly as disappearing from the employment record:

  • Hispanic graduates show the highest rate of persistently having no Minnesota employment record of any group examined — roughly double the rate for White graduates.
  • Asian/Pacific Islander graduates show a nearly identical elevated rate, also roughly double the White rate, and this disparity holds up independently even after accounting for every other factor in the analysis — making it one of the more robust and least-explained findings in this pathway.
  • Black graduates show elevated risk on both fronts, though their disadvantage is somewhat more concentrated in disappearing from the record (roughly 27% persistently no-record, compared to about 21% for White graduates).

This distinction matters for how these disparities should be understood: American Indian graduates’ disadvantage looks like an instability problem — cycling through unsteady work — while Hispanic and Asian/Pacific Islander graduates’ disadvantage looks much more like disconnection from Minnesota’s tracked labor market entirely. These are different situations that likely call for different kinds of outreach or support, even though both show up as “disparities in disconnection” at first glance.

Implications for Policy and Practice

Early instability often persists without intervention — but not to the same degree for every type of disconnection. Unstable employment tends to resolve on its own over time for many graduates. Disappearing entirely from the employment record does not resolve at the same rate — once established, it behaves like a durable state rather than a temporary setback, making it the more urgent target for early intervention.

Postsecondary exposure and structured credentials matter, even without completion. The clearest dividing line in this entire analysis is not which credential someone earns, but whether they engage with postsecondary education or workforce credentialing at all. Expanding access to postsecondary programs — even short, non-completed exposure — appears to meaningfully reduce both forms of disconnection.

CTE and non-degree pathways remain central for this population. Many graduates on this pathway are unlikely to pursue a four-year degree. Structured, employer-connected pathways that don’t require one continue to show some of the strongest protective effects identified in this analysis.

Different groups may need different kinds of support. Because disconnection splits into two distinct patterns — instability versus disappearance from the tracked labor market — and because different demographic groups are concentrated in different patterns, a single, undifferentiated equity strategy is unlikely to serve every group well. Groups facing an instability problem (Special Education status, economic disadvantage, American Indian graduates) may be better served by job-quality, retention, and advancement-focused supports. Groups facing a no-record problem (Hispanic and Asian/Pacific Islander graduates in particular) may require a different kind of outreach or investigation — one aimed at understanding whether these graduates have left the region, left the state, or become genuinely disconnected from the labor force altogether.
