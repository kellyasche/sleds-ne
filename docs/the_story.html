The Story

The Findings: Paths Taken by High School Graduates

In the year right after graduation, half of Northeast Minnesota graduates (50.4%) are attending post-secondary education, and only about 9% have reached meaningful employment anywhere in the state. That balance shifts fast. By six years out, meaningful employment in Minnesota climbs to nearly 45% of graduates, and employment within the region itself (same county or elsewhere in the EDR) reaches close to 28% — roughly where it plateaus through year eleven (28.9%).

Not every graduate lands in employment or education, though. The share of graduates without a Minnesota employment record and not enrolled in post-secondary grows steadily the whole time — from 11.9% in year one to 23.2% by year six to 33.4% by year eleven. By a decade out, this is the single largest group in the dataset. A separate share (18–29%) show some Minnesota employment record but haven’t cleared the “meaningful employment” bar; this group holds fairly steady around 20% after the first year or two.

The takeaway: post-secondary enrollment dominates the first year, employment builds steadily through year six, and the share of graduates falling out of Minnesota’s tracked systems altogether keeps growing the entire ten-plus years — it never resolves on its own.

Getting to Know the Pathways

Pathway 1: The College Completers’ Pathway

Completing college is highly associated with sustained labor force participation

42.5% of Northeast Minnesota high school graduates go on to complete a post-secondary credential. The remaining 57.5% either never attend or attend without finishing.

In year one, non-completers actually show higher regional employment (19.2% vs. 11.6% for completers) — unsurprising, since most completers are still in school (77.4% still attending post-secondary at year one). By year five, that gap closes almost entirely (26.9% completers vs. 26.3% non-completers in regional employment), but completers pull ahead in employment elsewhere in the state (18.0% vs. 12.6%). By year ten, completers hold a clear regional advantage (33.0% vs. 25.6%) and a larger statewide advantage (23.4% vs. 15.6%). Non-completers are consistently more likely to end up disconnected — by year ten, 36.7% have no Minnesota employment record, compared to 28.0% of completers.

The takeaway: completing college doesn’t produce an immediate employment advantage — it produces a long one. The payoff shows up over years, not months, and shows up as much in avoiding disconnection as in landing a job.

Who was more likely to complete a postsecondary program?

Controlling for other factors, the strongest barriers to completing college are structural, not academic:

  • Special Education status: 62% lower odds of completing (OR ≈ 0.38)
  • Economic disadvantage: 59% lower odds (OR ≈ 0.41)
  • American Indian students: 51% lower odds relative to White students (OR ≈ 0.49)
  • Black students: 46% lower odds (OR ≈ 0.54)
  • Hispanic students: 27% lower odds (OR ≈ 0.73)
  • Asian/Pacific Islander students: no meaningful difference from White students

Academic preparation moves the odds the other way: female students show 66% higher odds of completing (OR ≈ 1.66), PSEO participants show 44% higher odds (OR ≈ 1.44), and each additional point of MCA Math proficiency adds roughly 29% higher odds (OR ≈ 1.29). CTE participation, notably, has no independent effect on whether a student completes college at all — its influence shows up elsewhere, in workforce outcomes directly.

The takeaway: the biggest sorting in the entire education-to-workforce pipeline happens right here, at the completion stage — before geography, credential type, or anything else even enters the picture.

Where Do College Completers Participate in the Labor Force?

Among all graduates, completing college anywhere roughly doubles the odds of sustained regional employment relative to non-completers — but where that credential is earned matters far more than what credential is earned. Graduating from a postsecondary institution within the region is associated with 142–197% higher odds of sustained regional employment (OR ≈ 2.42–2.97, depending on model specification) — the single strongest predictor found anywhere in this project. Graduating elsewhere in Minnesota cuts those odds by about 44%; graduating outside Minnesota cuts them by about 36%.

Credential level looks powerful on its own — associate degrees and sub-baccalaureate credentials show roughly double the odds of sustained regional employment relative to non-completers — but once graduation location is added to the model, most of that effect disappears. A bachelor’s degree, meanwhile, shows no regional employment advantage at all once location is accounted for (and a modest disadvantage in the fully specified model). CTE participation remains an independent, positive predictor of sustained regional employment throughout, regardless of credential or location (16–38% higher odds depending on level of CTE engagement).

Overall, 59% of post-secondary completers finish their credential within the region, 24% finish elsewhere in Minnesota, and 17% finish outside Minnesota entirely.

The takeaway: geography, not credential depth, is the decisive workforce anchor. Regional colleges matter more to regional workforce retention than the type of degree students walk away with.

What high school experiences were associated with college location?

Among students who complete college, the students most likely to graduate within the region are the ones facing the most structural constraint: economically disadvantaged students (57% higher odds, OR ≈ 1.57), Special Education students (32% higher odds, OR ≈ 1.32), and American Indian students (45% higher odds, OR ≈ 1.45). CTE engagement also strongly predicts in-region completion — CTE concentrators/completers show 43% higher odds (OR ≈ 1.43).

The students most likely to complete outside the region are the most academically prepared: each additional point of MCA Math proficiency lowers the odds of in-region completion by about 21% (OR ≈ 0.79), PSEO participants show 11% lower odds (OR ≈ 0.89), and Black and Asian/Pacific Islander completers show meaningfully lower odds of graduating in-region (44% and 39% lower, respectively). Entirely rural high school districts see the starkest geographic sorting bivariately — only 38% of their graduates complete within the region, compared to 61% from urban/town-rural mix districts, with nearly half of rural graduates completing elsewhere in Minnesota instead.

The takeaway: the students the region is best positioned to retain are, on average, the students who had the fewest other options — and the students with the strongest academic preparation are consistently the ones most likely to leave.

Pathway 2: The Workforce-First Pathway

57.5% (roughly 58%) of Northeast Minnesota high school graduates never complete a post-secondary credential.

Understanding this group

Five years after high school, 38.9% of non-completers have sustained workforce participation somewhere in Minnesota — 26.3% within the region itself, and another 12.6% elsewhere in the state. That combined share has been climbing steadily since year one (24.8% at year one, projected to reach roughly 41% by year ten), which is a genuinely positive trend for a meaningful share of this population.

The takeaway: workforce-first graduates aren’t a monolith — nearly 4 in 10 build durable employment within five years, and that share keeps growing through year ten.

Who was more likely to stay connected to the regional workforce?

CTE engagement and having taken the ACT are the strongest positive predictors of staying regionally connected for this group: CTE concentrators/completers show 40% higher odds of sustained regional employment (OR ≈ 1.40), CTE participants show 20% higher odds (OR ≈ 1.20), and simply having taken the ACT — regardless of score — shows 178% higher odds (OR ≈ 2.78), though each additional point on the ACT itself is associated with slightly lower odds (OR ≈ 0.954 per point) — meaning that taking the test at all matters more than how well a student scored.

Racial disparities are pronounced and don’t attenuate: Black graduates show 55% lower odds of sustained regional employment (OR ≈ 0.45), Hispanic graduates show 39% lower odds (OR ≈ 0.61), and American Indian graduates show 20% lower odds (OR ≈ 0.80). Female graduates in this group show 15% lower odds than male graduates (OR ≈ 0.85) — notably the opposite direction from the college-completion pathway, where women had a clear advantage. Economic disadvantage, Special Education status, and English learner status are each independently associated with modestly lower odds as well.

The takeaway: for graduates who don’t complete college, CTE participation and ACT participation (not score) are the clearest levers associated with staying regionally connected — but the same racial disparities seen elsewhere in the pipeline persist here too, unexplained by preparation or geography.

Pathway 3: The Disconnected Pathway

Size of this group

Five years after high school, 56.9% of non-completers are disconnected from sustained workforce participation — 27.3% in non-sustained employment and 29.6% with no Minnesota employment record at all.

Persistently Disconnected

Looking across all five years at once (rather than one year at a time) sharpens this picture considerably. 53.2% of non-completers are persistently disconnected — disconnected in 4 or 5 of their first 5 years out of school. Only 11.9% are consistently attached in every one of those years — a group the exact same size as the smallest disconnection category (“broadly disconnected,” also 11.9%). Combined, nearly two-thirds of non-completers (65.1%) spend at least three of their first five years disconnected.

The takeaway: disconnection isn’t a brief early-career phase that most people age out of — for a majority of non-completers, it’s the dominant, years-long experience.

Two Kinds of Disconnection

“Disconnection” is really two distinct and oppositely-trending phenomena. Non-sustained employment — unstable work, but still present in the tracked system — shrinks steadily over time, from 43.4% of non-completers in year one to 26.4% by year five. It behaves like a transitional condition: most people who experience it move through it. Separately measured across the full 5-year window, only 15.7% of non-completers get stuck in non-sustained employment for the bulk of their first five years, while the largest single group (39.9%) experiences it only briefly (1–2 years).

No Minnesota employment record, on the other hand, grows every single year — from 26.4% in year one to 33.4% by year five, becoming the single largest outcome of any category. It behaves like a stable end-state rather than a passing phase: just over half of non-completers (51.0%) never fall off Minnesota’s tracked system at all, while 21.3% are persistently absent from it — a genuinely bimodal, not front-loaded, distribution.

Breaking down the “persistently disconnected” group itself confirms these are different populations experiencing different problems: 40.1% of persistently disconnected graduates got there primarily by disappearing from Minnesota’s tracked employment system, 29.5% got there through genuinely unstable — but still present and trackable — employment, and the remaining 30.4% alternate between the two, never accumulating enough years in either single category to show up in a mechanism-specific analysis on its own.

The takeaway: non-sustained employment and no-Minnesota-record aren’t two flavors of the same problem — they trend in opposite directions, and roughly 4 in 10 persistently disconnected graduates are disconnected specifically because they’ve fallen out of the state’s tracked system, not because their jobs were unstable.

A Local Opportunity

Among graduates in non-sustained employment, the large majority remain at least partially attached to the regional labor market throughout the five-year window — 81.1% in year one, declining gradually but staying above 73% through year five. Only a small, flat share (7–8% of the total non-completer population) works entirely outside the region with no regional component at all, and that share doesn’t grow over time.

The takeaway: this is a story of regional underutilization, not regional flight. The people captured in non-sustained employment are overwhelmingly present and working in the region — just not steadily or sufficiently to clear the “meaningful employment” threshold. The real long-term risk isn’t people leaving the region for work elsewhere; it’s people disappearing from the tracked system entirely.

Who is Most at Risk of Disconnection?

Persistent non-sustained employment (instability): Special Education status is the clearest, most stable risk factor found anywhere in this analysis — a real, persistent predictor of instability (OR ≈ 1.36–1.45) that holds up through every level of statistical control. Female graduates (OR ≈ 1.32), American Indian graduates (OR ≈ 1.37–1.39), and Black graduates (OR ≈ 1.68–1.74) all show consistently elevated risk. PSEO participation, typically protective elsewhere in this project, shows a small but real adverse association with instability specifically (OR ≈ 1.21–1.22).

Persistent no-Minnesota-employment-record: English learner status is the single clearest finding in this entire analysis — English learners show nearly double the odds of persistently disappearing from Minnesota’s tracked employment system (OR ≈ 1.71), and that risk holds up fully even after accounting for high school accomplishments and postsecondary attempt. Hispanic graduates (OR ≈ 2.29) and Asian/Pacific Islander graduates (OR ≈ 2.12–2.24) show the largest racial disparities found anywhere in this project — both close to or above double the odds of White graduates. Black graduates are also consistently elevated (OR ≈ 1.41–1.47).

Notably, Asian/Pacific Islander graduates show a striking split between the two mechanisms: they have lower odds of instability but among the highest odds of falling out of the tracked system entirely — a reminder that “disconnection” means genuinely different things for different groups.

The takeaway: the two disconnection mechanisms put different students at risk. Special Education status and being American Indian point toward instability; English learner status and being Hispanic or Asian/Pacific Islander point toward disappearing from the record altogether. A single, combined “at risk” profile would miss both stories.

Disparities Compound Across the Pipeline

The disparities that show up in disconnection don’t start there — they start at college completion. Special Education students (62% lower odds of completing college), economically disadvantaged students (59% lower odds), American Indian students (51% lower odds), Black students (46% lower odds), and Hispanic students (27% lower odds) are all substantially less likely to complete a postsecondary credential in the first place, well before disconnection risk is even measured.

The takeaway: the same groups facing the steepest barriers to college completion are, in several cases, the same groups facing the steepest disconnection risk later on. Disadvantage isn’t introduced at any single stage of this pipeline — it accumulates across it.

What Offers Protection Against Disconnection?

Protections Against Persistent Instability

Attempting postsecondary education — even without completing it — is the clearest protective factor against persistent instability found in this analysis. Students who ever attended post-secondary show 29% lower odds of persistent non-sustained employment (OR ≈ 0.715), and this effect survives every level of statistical control, holding up independently alongside CTE, PSEO, and ACT participation. Asian/Pacific Islander graduates also show meaningfully reduced odds of instability specifically (OR ≈ 0.55–0.60).

Protections Against Persistent No MN Employment Record

Having taken the ACT is the single most powerful protective factor found anywhere in this project — students who took the ACT show 70–74% lower odds of persistently disappearing from Minnesota’s tracked employment system (OR ≈ 0.26–0.30), regardless of the score they earned. Attempting postsecondary education is also strongly protective here, and even more so than for instability (OR ≈ 0.53, a 47% reduction in odds). PSEO participation (OR ≈ 0.75–0.76) and CTE achievement (OR ≈ 0.69–0.75) both show consistent, independent protective effects against this mechanism specifically.

The takeaway: the protective story mirrors the risk story — different levers protect against different mechanisms. Postsecondary attempt protects against both, but taking the ACT and participating in PSEO or CTE protect specifically against falling out of Minnesota’s tracked system, while they do little or nothing to prevent instability on their own.
