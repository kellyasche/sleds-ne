Multivariate Confirmation Analysis: Logistic Regression

The purpose of this project is to explain which characteristics are associated with entry into distinct workforce participation pathways following high school graduation in Northeast Minnesota (EDR 3). These pathways include sustained employment in the region, sustained employment elsewhere in Minnesota, non-sustained employment, and having no Minnesota employment record.

The policy question of greatest interest is what factors are associated with sustained employment in the region. Earlier analysis relied on bivariate analyses (cross-tabulations, Cramér’s V, and ANOVA) to identify patterns of stratification across workforce participation outcomes. A consistent finding was that postsecondary experiences—particularly credential attainment and where education was completed—are strongly associated with long-term workforce attachment. Academic preparation, early college exposure (PSEO), career and technical education (CTE), and structural demographic factors also demonstrated meaningful relationships.

To strengthen confidence in these findings, a limited multivariate analysis is conducted using logistic regression. The purpose of this modeling is not to establish causality or build a predictive tool, but to examine whether the strongest bivariate relationships remain directionally consistent when key variables are considered simultaneously. This approach helps clarify whether preparation variables retain independent associations after accounting for credential attainment, and whether regional postsecondary completion remains influential when controlling for background and readiness.

Logistic regression is used here as a confirmatory tool rather than the primary analytic framework.


Methodology

Dependent Variables

This analysis models five distinct outcomes, each restricted to the appropriate subset of the cohort:

  1. Sustained regional workforce attachment (grad.year.5) — binary (1 = meaningful employment within the region five years after high school graduation, 0 = otherwise), modeled across the full graduating cohort.
  2. Sustained regional workforce attachment (grad.year.5, college completers only) - binary (1 = meaningful employment within the region five years after high school graduation, 0 = otherwise), modeled across college completers only.
  3. Postsecondary completion (ps.grad) — binary (1 = completed a postsecondary credential, 0 = did not), modeled across all high school graduates, regardless of whether they ever attended postsecondary education.
  4. Postsecondary completion within the region — binary (1 = completed a postsecondary credential within the region, 0 = completed elsewhere in Minnesota or out of state), restricted to postsecondary completers only.
  5. Sub-baccalaureate credential attainment — binary (1 = highest credential earned is an associate degree or less-than-associate credential, 0 = bachelor’s degree or higher), restricted to postsecondary completers only.

Sample

All four models are restricted to the 2011–2023 graduating cohorts. Earlier cohorts (2008–2010) are excluded because PSEO participation was not consistently tracked in the data system prior to 2011 — missingness in that variable is concentrated almost entirely in those years rather than distributed randomly, and including them would risk misclassifying genuine PSEO participants as non-participants. And PSEO participation was identified as having a very strong relationship to our dependent variables in the bivariate analysis.

We will also be eliminate graduation years between 2020 and 2023 due to significant amounts of “Unknown” categorized in the RaceEthnicty variable.

Independent Variables

Variables were selected based on (1) a demonstrated bivariate relationship with the relevant outcome(s) in the cross-tabulation and ANOVA analysis, and (2) practical usability in a multivariate model, with closely related variables consolidated to avoid multicollinearity. The same variable set and coding is used across all four outcomes.

Demographics

  • Gender
  • RaceEthnicity
  • economic.status
  • SpecialEdStatus
  • english.learner

High school characteristics

  • Dem_Desc (district rural/urban classification)
  • range.school
  • avg.wages.pct.state (local average wages as a percentage of the state average)
  • avg.unemp.rate (local average unemployment rate)

High school accomplishments

  • pseo.participant (Post-Secondary Enrollment Options participation)
  • took.ACT (whether the student took the ACT)
  • ACTCompositeScore (ACT composite score, recoded to 0 for non-test-takers — see note below)
  • ap.exam (AP exam participation)
  • sat.taken
  • cte.achievement (CTE concentration/completion level)
  • total.cte.courses.taken

Postsecondary variables

  • ps.grad - used only for the DV1a WF - Region as the dependent variable with full population
  • highest.cred.level - these will be tested on the dependent variables where college completers are the only individuals being analysed.
  • ps.grad.location - these will be tested on the dependent variables where college completers are the only individuals being analysed.

Notes on Variable Selection

Academic achievement (MCA). MCA Math, Reading, and Science scores are highly correlated with one another (r = 0.63–0.75) and with ACT performance, and all three are excluded from this analysis. MCA Math’s missingness rate differs sharply by English learner status (26.1% vs. 2.4% for other students), and this pattern cannot be reliably classified as an independent confounder versus a mediator of unequal access, resourcing, or administrative practice across student populations. Controlling for it — whether through the raw score or a missingness indicator — risks statistically absorbing a real disparity under the guise of a data-quality correction. No MCA subject is retained as a substitute for another; the concern applies to the construct as a whole, not to Math specifically.

CTE engagement. cte.achievement (concentrator/completer/participant/no CTE) is retained over total.cte.courses.taken, a continuous measure of the same underlying construct. The two are redundant, and the categorical version is more directly interpretable for a non-technical audience.

ACT and SAT participation and score. Students who did not take the ACT have no composite score by definition — this is a structural absence tied entirely to an observed variable (took.ACT), not missing data in the conventional sense. To retain the full sample rather than dropping non-test-takers, ACTCompositeScore is recoded to 0 for non-test-takers and entered into the model alongside took.ACT. With both terms present, took.ACT captures the effect of test participation itself, while ACTCompositeScore captures the achievement gradient specifically among test-takers, without the two effects being confounded. sat.taken is entered separately, capturing a distinct, smaller subgroup of test-takers not fully overlapping with ACT participation. Because took.ACT shows a participation gap by English learner status (41.4% vs. 11.2%) of a similar scale to the MCA missingness gap that led to MCA’s exclusion, any attenuation of the English learner coefficient once took.ACT enters the model should be interpreted with the same caution — as a variable that may partly reflect unequal access rather than a fully independent behavior — rather than treated as the disparity being explained away.

District and school context. range.school is included alongside Dem_Desc; both show real, stable bivariate relationships with the outcomes examined and are treated as distinct measures of school/community context rather than redundant with one another.

Variables dropped. total.cte.courses.taken, MCA.M, MCA.R, and MCA.S are excluded — the CTE course-count measure due to redundancy with cte.achievement, and all three MCA subjects for the reasons described above.

Modeling Approach

Each outcome is modeled using a sequential block-entry approach, adding one group of predictors at a time to assess the relative contribution of each. Each block is compared to the prior one using a likelihood ratio test to confirm it adds significant explanatory power, with AIC and pseudo-R² tracked across the sequence to summarize overall model improvement.

  • Model 1: Demographics
  • Model 2: + High school characteristics
  • Model 3: + High school accomplishments
  • Model 4: + College completion

Sustained regional workforce attachment - college completers only

  • Model 1: Demographics
  • Model 2: + High school characteristics
  • Model 3: + High school accomplishments

This model adds PSEO participation, ACT participation and score, SAT participation, AP exam participation, and CTE achievement to the growing specification (n = 20,198).

English learner status is a significant, independent predictor of reduced regional attachment — the central finding of this model. OR ≈ 0.741 (CI: 0.629-0.872, p < .001), meaning English learners show roughly 26% lower odds of sustained regional workforce attachment, holding up fully alongside every high school accomplishment variable in the model. This is not explained away by academic engagement or preparation.

Race/Ethnicity effects persist across all four categories, fully independent of high school accomplishments. American Indian (OR ≈ 0.85, p = .012), Asian/Pacific Islander (OR ≈ 0.66, p = .006), Black (OR ≈ 0.43, p < .001), and Hispanic graduates (OR ≈ 0.59, p < .001) all show significantly lower odds of sustained regional attachment relative to White graduates — none of these disparities are explained by academic engagement patterns.

CTE achievement continues to anchor students regionally. CTE Concentrators/Completers show 42% higher odds of sustained regional attachment (OR ≈ 1.42, p < .001), and CTE Participants show 22% higher odds (OR ≈ 1.22, p < .001) — the clearest “stay local” signal in the model.

ACT shows a genuine crossover pattern that requires a joint reading. Taking the ACT is associated with substantially higher odds of regional attachment (OR ≈ 3.77, p < .001), while among test-takers, each additional composite point is associated with lower odds (OR ≈ 0.938, p < .001). Together these describe a threshold effect: simply engaging with the ACT is not a signal of leaving the region — it’s specifically high-scoring ACT engagement that predicts departure, likely reflecting pursuit of selective, non-regional postsecondary options.

SAT participation moves in the opposite direction from ACT participation — a real, substantively important contrast, not a contradiction. SAT-takers show 60% lower odds of regional attachment (OR ≈ 0.40, p < .001). Unlike the ACT, which nearly every college-bound student in this region takes, SAT-taking identifies a smaller, more selective subgroup specifically oriented toward postsecondary options outside Minnesota — consistent with the bivariate finding that SAT-takers are the most mobile subgroup in this analysis.

AP exam participation is associated with lower odds of regional attachment (OR ≈ 0.71, p < .001), consistent with the pattern that college-preparatory engagement pulls graduates away from the region.

PSEO participation is not statistically significant here (OR ≈ 1.04, CI: 0.969-1.111, p = .290) — its bivariate influence does not hold up as an independent effect once ACT, SAT, AP, and CTE are considered together.

Economic status and Special Education status both strengthen rather than attenuate. Economic status returns to clear significance (OR ≈ 0.86, p < .001), reversing the marginal result from Model 2. Special Ed status also strengthens (OR ≈ 0.77, p < .001, down from OR ≈ 0.85 in Model 2). Neither effect is explained by academic engagement — if anything, accounting for it sharpens both.

Range school status and Gender both weaken to non-significance. Range school (OR ≈ 1.06, p = .136) and Gender (OR ≈ 0.94, p = .069) were both significant in Model 2 but fall short of conventional significance here — suggesting their apparent relationships with regional attachment may run substantially through students’ patterns of academic and CTE engagement rather than operating independently.

Average local wages becomes significant for the first time (OR ≈ 2.20, CI: 1.139-4.248, p = .019) — higher local wages relative to the state average now show a real, if modest, positive relationship with regional attachment once accomplishment patterns are accounted for. Model 1: Demographics

This model estimates the association between demographic characteristics and sustained regional workforce attachment five years after high school graduation, restricted to postsecondary completers (n = 8,500). The outcome is coded 1 = sustained workforce attachment within the region (“Sustained WF - NE”), 0 = all other outcomes.

This model tells a meaningfully different story than DV1a’s demographics-only model, not just a smaller-sample version of the same one. Several effects reverse direction entirely once the population is restricted to college completers.

  • Economic status: OR ≈ 1.21 (CI: 1.091–1.340) — economically disadvantaged completers show higher odds of sustained regional attachment. This is the opposite direction from DV1a, where economic disadvantage predicted lower odds across the full cohort.
  • Special Education status: OR ≈ 1.40 (CI: 1.133–1.740) — also reversed from DV1a, where Special Ed status predicted lower odds.

Some effects persist in the same direction as DV1a, though attenuated:

  • Gender: OR ≈ 0.89 (CI: 0.815–0.975) — female completers show modestly lower odds, consistent with DV1a.

Overall Interpretation

Among students who complete a postsecondary credential, the demographic predictors of sustained regional workforce attachment look substantially different than they do across the full graduating cohort. Economic disadvantage and Special Education status, both associated with lower regional attachment in the full-cohort model, are associated with higher attachment once the population is restricted to completers — suggesting that among students who make it through college, these groups may be more likely to return to or remain in the region, possibly reflecting stronger local ties, more localized program/institution choices, or different postsecondary pathways than their more economically advantaged or non-Special-Ed peers. Race/Ethnicity effects, particularly for Asian/Pacific Islander and Black graduates, remain directionally consistent with the full-cohort findings, suggesting those disparities are not solely a function of who completes college but persist even after completion. English learner status, notably, shows no effect at all in this population — a genuine shift from its significance in every DV1a model, worth naming explicitly rather than attributing to sample size alone, since the coefficient itself moved toward the null, not just its precision.

Given the smaller sample size (n = 8,500 vs. 20,198), and especially the very small Hispanic subgroup (n = 65) in this population, some of these shifts should be interpreted cautiously — reduced power alone could account for lost significance in a few cases, but the reversals in direction for economic status, Special Ed, and American Indian graduates are unlikely to be explained by power alone and appear to reflect a genuinely different pattern among completers specifically.

This model adds highest.cred.level (Associate or lesser credential vs. Bachelor’s degree or higher) to the full specification, restricted to postsecondary completers (n = 8,500).

Credential level is a strong, independent predictor of regional attachment — the standout finding in this block. Graduates whose highest credential is an Associate degree or less show 78% higher odds of sustained regional attachment than those with a Bachelor’s degree or higher (OR ≈ 1.78, CI: 1.594-1.985, p < .001). Took-ACT’s effect shrinks meaningfully once credential level is added, though it remains large and highly significant. OR drops from ≈ 4.28 in Model 3 to ≈ 3.25 here (p < .001 in both) — credential level is absorbing some, but not all, of what ACT participation was capturing, which makes sense given how closely ACT engagement tracks with pursuing a Bachelor’s degree specifically.

Local wages becomes significant for the first time in this sequence. OR ≈ 2.90 (CI: 1.042-8.020, p = .041) — a real, if modest, positive relationship between local wage strength and regional attachment once credential level is accounted for, crossing the threshold it had only approached in Model 3 (p = .051).

All demographic effects that were non-significant in Model 3 remain non-significant here. Gender (p = .629), economic status (p = .559), Special Education status (p = .435), English learner status (p = .572), American Indian graduates (p = .132), and Range school status (p = .314) all continue to show no independent relationship with regional attachment among completers, once credential level joins academic accomplishments in the model.

District classification, ACT composite score, SAT, AP exam, and CTE achievement all remain significant and essentially stable in direction and magnitude from Model 3 — none of these relationships are meaningfully altered by adding credential level to the specification.

Adding highest credential earned significantly improves model fit (likelihood ratio test: Δdeviance = 105.85, df = 1, p < .001; AIC decreases from 10,657.8 to 10,553.9), confirming credential level as a large, genuine, independent predictor of regional attachment among completers — sub-baccalaureate credential holders are substantially more likely to stay regionally attached than Bachelor’s-degree holders. English learner status remains a clean null throughout this entire sequence, consistent with every prior model in the completers-only population. The demographic effects that lost significance in Model 3 — economic status, Special Ed, American Indian identity, Gender, and Range school status — remain non-significant here as well, reinforcing that their earlier relationships with regional attachment ran through academic engagement and credential level, not through the characteristics themselves.

DV1b Summary: What Predicts Sustained Regional Workforce Attachment (College Completers Only)

Across all five models, this analysis identifies a fundamentally different pattern of predictors than the full-population model — the same outcome, but a genuinely distinct story once the population is restricted to students who completed a postsecondary credential.

Postsecondary graduation location is the single largest predictor identified in this entire analysis, full population included. Graduates who completed their credential within the region show more than triple the odds of sustained regional workforce attachment compared to those who completed elsewhere in Minnesota (OR ≈ 3.22, p < .001) — an effect larger than any demographic, academic, or credential-level variable in either model sequence.

English learner status shows no relationship with regional attachment at any stage of this sequence — a clean, stable null across all five models. This stands in sharp contrast to the full-population model, where English learner status was one of the most robust and consequential findings identified. Among students who go on to complete a postsecondary credential, whatever drives reduced regional attachment for English learners in the broader population does not appear to operate.

Highest credential level is a strong, independent predictor, though much of its apparent strength runs through where that credential was earned. Sub-baccalaureate credential holders show substantially higher odds of regional attachment than Bachelor’s-degree holders throughout (OR ≈ 1.78 before graduation location is considered, OR ≈ 1.40 once it is) — a real, independent effect that survives full specification, but one that is partly explained by the fact that associate-level and technical credentials are disproportionately regional.

Race/Ethnicity findings differ meaningfully by specification, and one group’s disparity only emerges in the fully specified model. Black graduates show a consistent, significant disadvantage across every model (OR ≈ 0.47–0.57). Hispanic graduates remain marginal through four models before becoming significant only in the full specification (OR ≈ 0.55, p = .044) — their disadvantage among completers is real but only detectable once location, credential level, and full academic engagement are jointly accounted for. Asian/Pacific Islander graduates remain consistently at the edge of significance throughout (never quite crossing the threshold in the fully controlled models). American Indian graduates tell the most distinct story of any group: they show significantly higher odds of regional attachment in the demographics-only and characteristics models — the opposite direction from the full-population finding — before fading to non-significance once academic accomplishments enter the specification.

Economic status, Special Education status, and Gender all show a positive relationship with regional attachment early in the sequence, then lose significance once academic accomplishments are considered. All three are significant, protective predictors in the demographics-only and characteristics models, but none hold up independently once ACT, SAT, AP, and CTE engagement are in the model — their apparent advantage among completers appears to run through how these groups engage academically in high school, not through the characteristics themselves.

The ACT crossover pattern replicates precisely, and mirrors the full- population finding exactly. Taking the ACT is associated with substantially higher odds of regional attachment throughout the sequence, while among test-takers, each additional composite point predicts lower odds — the same threshold effect found in the full population: engagement itself is protective, while high-scoring engagement specifically predicts leaving the region. Both effects shrink somewhat once credential level and graduation location are added, as those variables absorb some of what ACT participation and score were capturing.

SAT participation and AP exam participation both show a consistent negative relationship with regional attachment, weakening somewhat by the full specification but remaining significant throughout — consistent with the full-population finding that these measures identify a subgroup oriented toward postsecondary and career options outside the region.

CTE achievement continues to anchor students regionally across every model in this sequence, holding significant and stable in both category levels from the point it enters the specification.

PSEO participation shows no relationship with regional attachment at any stage — consistent with the full-population model, where PSEO’s strong bivariate influence likewise failed to hold up once other academic accomplishment variables were considered.

District classification and local economic conditions are substantially explained by graduation location. Urban/town/rural mix district classification remains significant throughout, but Town/rural mix loses significance once graduation location is added — its apparent advantage runs largely through where its graduates attend postsecondary. Local wages briefly becomes significant once credential level is added, then loses significance entirely once graduation location is controlled for — the wage effect was substantially a proxy for regional postsecondary attendance, not an independent local-economy relationship. Local unemployment rate and Range school status remain non-significant from the point academic accomplishments enter the model onward.

Model 1: Demographics

This model estimates the association between demographic characteristics and postsecondary completion, modeled across all high school graduates regardless of whether they ever attended postsecondary education (n = 26,327). The outcome is coded 1 = completed a postsecondary credential, 0 = did not.

Economic status and Special Education status show the largest effects of any demographic predictor examined in this project so far.

  • Economic disadvantage: approximately 64% lower odds of postsecondary completion relative to non-disadvantaged students (OR ≈ 0.36).
  • Special Education status: approximately 73% lower odds of completion (OR ≈ 0.27).

Both are dramatically larger effects than anything seen in the DV1a/DV1b sequences, where these same variables showed effects in the 10–40% range. This makes sense given what the variable is measuring — completion itself is a much more direct, proximate outcome of the barriers these statuses represent than downstream regional employment is.

Race and ethnicity shows substantial disparities across every category.

  • American Indian graduates: approximately 44% lower odds of completion relative to White graduates (OR ≈ 0.56).
  • Black graduates: approximately 48% lower odds (OR ≈ 0.52).
  • Hispanic graduates: approximately 43% lower odds (OR ≈ 0.57).
  • Asian/Pacific Islander graduates: not statistically significant (OR ≈ 0.86, CI: 0.682–1.072, crosses 1) — the only Race/Ethnicity category without a significant gap in this model.
  • Unknown race/ethnicity: approximately 84% lower odds (OR ≈ 0.16) — given the very small residual size of this category (n ≈ 40) and the fact that “Unknown” doesn’t correspond to any real demographic group, this coefficient should not be substantively interpreted; it is retained in the model for completeness but carries no meaningful narrative value.

English learner status is associated with substantially lower odds of completion (OR ≈ 0.66) — a much larger effect here than in any DV1a/DV1b model, consistent with postsecondary completion being a more proximate outcome for this predictor as well.

Gender shows a notably different pattern than in any regional-employment model.

  • Female graduates: approximately 74% higher odds of postsecondary completion relative to male graduates (OR ≈ 1.74) — a clear reversal from DV1a/DV1b, where female graduates showed modestly lower odds of regional workforce attachment. This is a substantively important finding: female graduates complete college at meaningfully higher rates, even though (per DV1a) they are somewhat less likely to remain regionally employed afterward.

Overall Interpretation

At the demographics-only stage, economic status and Special Education status emerge as the two strongest predictors of postsecondary completion examined anywhere in this project, far exceeding their effect sizes in the regional-employment models. Race/Ethnicity gaps are present across nearly every category. The gender reversal — women completing college at notably higher rates while showing modestly lower regional workforce attachment — is a pattern worth carrying forward as high school characteristics and accomplishments are added, since it suggests completion and regional attachment may be driven by partially distinct mechanisms for this demographic factor specifically.

This is a baseline model only — it does not yet account for high school context or academic preparation, both added in subsequent blocks.

Model fit: AIC = 32,334.4; n = 26,327.

Model 2: Demographics + High School Characteristics

This model adds district rural/urban classification, Range school status, local average wages, and local average unemployment rate to the demographic baseline (n = 26,327).

Range school status shows a strong, significant positive relationship with postsecondary completion — a genuinely different pattern than seen in the workforce-attachment models. Range graduates show 34% higher odds of completing a postsecondary credential than non-Range graduates (OR ≈ 1.34, CI: 1.254-1.438, p < .001). In the regional-attachment sequences, Range status was about staying local; here, it’s associated with a genuinely different outcome — completing postsecondary education at all, regardless of where.

Local wages and unemployment rate both become significant here, and in directions that tell a coherent “opportunity cost” story. Higher local wages relative to the state average are associated with lower odds of postsecondary completion (OR ≈ 0.53, CI: 0.299-0.930, p = .027) — where local jobs pay comparatively well, the incentive to pursue further education appears to weaken. Higher local unemployment is associated with higher odds of completion (OR ≈ 1.03 per unit, CI: 1.005-1.048, p = .015) — where local job prospects are weaker, students appear more likely to pursue a credential instead. This is the first model in this entire project where both local economic indicators show a real, intuitive, significant relationship simultaneously.

District classification shows only a marginal relationship here. Urban/town/rural mix districts show higher odds of completion that just misses conventional significance (OR ≈ 1.13, p = .053), and Town/rural mix is clearly non-significant (OR ≈ 1.10, p = .129) — a much weaker relationship than Dem_Desc showed for regional workforce attachment.

All five demographic effects from Model 1 remain essentially unchanged in direction and magnitude. Economic status (OR ≈ 0.36), Special Education status (OR ≈ 0.26), English learner status (OR ≈ 0.67), and the Race/Ethnicity pattern (American Indian, Black, and Hispanic all significantly lower than White; Asian/Pacific Islander non-significant) all hold steady from Model 1 — none of the demographic disparities in postsecondary completion are explained by district context, Range school status, or local economic conditions.

Overall Interpretation

Adding high school characteristics significantly improves model fit (likelihood ratio test: Δdeviance = 81.68, df = 5, p < .001; AIC decreases from 32,334.4 to 32,262.7). Range school status and local economic conditions are the standout findings in this block — Range status is associated with substantially higher odds of completing a credential at all (a different relationship than its “stay local” role in the workforce- attachment models), and local wages and unemployment both show coherent, significant relationships with completion for the first time in this project, consistent with an opportunity-cost interpretation. District classification, by contrast, shows only a weak, largely non-significant relationship with completion — geography matters far less here than it does for where graduates end up working.

DV4 Summary: What Predicts Sub-Baccalaureate Credential Attainment (College Completers Only)

Across all three models, academic accomplishments emerge as overwhelmingly the dominant force shaping whether a postsecondary completer earns a Bachelor’s degree or an Associate-or-lesser credential — far more so than demographics or geography, a genuinely different pattern than found in any other DV in this project. Recall the outcome is coded 1 = Associate degree or less, 0 = Bachelor’s degree or higher, so all odds ratios below describe the odds of the sub-baccalaureate outcome unless noted otherwise.

ACT engagement produces the single largest coefficient found anywhere in this entire analysis, and shows the clearest crossover pattern of any DV. Simply taking the ACT is associated with dramatically higher odds of completing at the sub-baccalaureate level (OR ≈ 14.12) — an enormous effect, even relative to the large ACT effects found elsewhere in this project. Among test-takers, the direction reverses: each additional composite point predicts substantially lower odds of a sub-baccalaureate credential (OR ≈ 0.816 per point) — higher scorers complete Bachelor’s degrees instead. Read together: ACT engagement without strong performance predicts a shorter, more technical credential pathway, while strong performance predicts the opposite.

Special Education status shows the single largest demographic effect found anywhere in this project, though it attenuates substantially once academic accomplishments are considered. Special Ed completers show over five times the odds of a sub-baccalaureate credential in the demographics-only and characteristics models (OR ≈ 5.19–5.31), weakening to just over double the odds once academic engagement is accounted for (OR ≈ 2.16) — a real, substantial reduction, but the effect remains large and clearly significant throughout.

Economic status shows a similarly large, stable pattern. Economically disadvantaged completers show substantially higher odds of a sub- baccalaureate credential across all three models (OR ≈ 1.90–2.54), weakening somewhat once academic accomplishments enter the model but remaining one of the strongest and most consistent predictors in this sequence.

English learner status is significant early in the sequence but resolves to a clean, genuine null once academic accomplishments are considered — with no missingness control anywhere in the specification. Significant in the demographics-only and characteristics models (OR ≈ 1.72–1.73, predicting higher odds of a sub-baccalaureate credential), English learner status loses significance entirely in the final model (OR ≈ 0.84). Whatever drives this early relationship appears to run through academic engagement rather than English learner status operating independently.

Race/Ethnicity findings shift meaningfully across the sequence. Asian/Pacific Islander graduates show a large, stable, significant relationship with Bachelor’s-level attainment throughout all three models (OR ≈ 0.32–0.39) — the most consistent Race/Ethnicity finding in this sequence. American Indian graduates are significant in the demographics- only and characteristics models (OR ≈ 1.44) but lose significance once academic accomplishments are added (OR ≈ 1.09). Black graduates show the opposite trajectory: non-significant in the first two models, then significant in the final model (OR ≈ 0.55) — a disparity favoring Bachelor’s attainment that only emerges once academic engagement is fully accounted for. Hispanic graduates remain non-significant throughout.

PSEO participation shows a distinct role for this outcome compared to every other DV in this project. OR ≈ 0.66 — PSEO participants are notably more likely to complete a Bachelor’s degree specifically, a different relationship than PSEO showed for completion generally or for regional attachment, where it predicted little or showed a different direction entirely.

CTE achievement shows its strongest positive effect anywhere in this project. CTE Concentrator/Completer status (OR ≈ 1.61) is the largest CTE coefficient found in any model sequence, with CTE Participant status showing a smaller but still significant effect (OR ≈ 1.14) — both substantively sensible, since CTE pathways are designed to lead directly into Associate-level and technical credentials.

AP exam and SAT participation both predict Bachelor’s-level attainment, consistent with their role throughout this project (AP: OR ≈ 0.74; SAT: OR ≈ 0.61).

Gender shows a large, stable, significant relationship throughout, with female completers consistently more likely to earn a Bachelor’s degree (OR ≈ 0.65–0.73 across all three models).

District classification shows no relationship with credential level at any stage — a sharp contrast with this project’s findings on completion location, where geography was one of the strongest predictors. Range school status is significant in the characteristics model (OR ≈ 1.57) but weakens to only marginal significance once academic accomplishments are considered (OR ≈ 1.13).

Local wages and unemployment rate both show effects that emerge or fade unpredictably across the sequence, worth treating as modest and provisional rather than forcing into a single coherent story. Local wages is significant and protective toward Bachelor’s attainment in the characteristics model (OR ≈ 0.29) but loses significance once academic accomplishments are added (OR ≈ 0.56). Local unemployment rate is non- significant until the final model, where it becomes significant in a counterintuitive direction (OR ≈ 0.89 per unit — higher unemployment predicting Bachelor’s attainment rather than the reverse) — worth noting plainly rather than over-explaining.


Executive Summary

This analysis used logistic regression to confirm and refine patterns identified in earlier descriptive work on post-high school pathways for Northeast Minnesota graduates. Five models were built, each isolating a different piece of the pathway: whether graduates stay in the region long term, whether they complete college, where they complete it, and what type of credential they earn.

Four consistent themes emerged across all five models:

  1. Geography does different jobs at different stages. High school district type, Range school status, and local economic conditions strongly predict whether a graduate stays in the region for work and where they complete their postsecondary education — but have almost no bearing on whether they complete college at all, or what type of credential they earn once they do. Geography shapes where things happen, not whether or what.

  2. CTE and ACT represent two distinct, opposing pathways, and this project’s clearest, largest, and most consistent finding. Career and technical education consistently predicts staying in the region and completing shorter, more technical credentials. ACT engagement shows a striking split, visible in every model: simply taking the test predicts staying regional and completing an Associate-level credential — in one model, taking the ACT alone predicts fourteen times higher odds of a sub-baccalaureate credential — while scoring well on it predicts leaving the region and completing a Bachelor’s degree instead. These aren’t two versions of the same “college-readiness” signal; they represent different, sometimes opposing, routes through the pipeline.

  3. English learner status tells two entirely different stories, depending on which outcome is examined — one of the most important findings in this analysis. For the full graduating population, English learner status is a robust, independent predictor of reduced odds of sustained regional workforce attachment, holding up fully alongside demographics, geography, academic accomplishments, and postsecondary completion — this is a real, standalone disparity, not explained by anything else in the model. But among students who go on to complete a postsecondary credential, English learner status shows no relationship at all with regional attachment, completion location, or credential level. In other words: the English learner disparity in this data is entirely about workforce attachment for those who don’t complete a credential — it is not a story about academic preparation, and it disappears entirely once a student completes college.

  4. Demographic disparities are real, but some are explained by how students engage with school, while others are not. Economic disadvantage and Special Education status both show very large effects early in the postsecondary-completion and credential-level models, and both weaken substantially — though remain significant — once academic engagement is added, indicating that some, not all, of the disparity operates through unequal access to or engagement with CTE, PSEO, and testing pathways. Black, Hispanic, and Asian/Pacific Islander graduates tell a different story: their disparities in regional workforce attachment and postsecondary completion largely persist regardless of academic engagement, and in several models a disparity only becomes visible once academic engagement is fully accounted for — these are not explained away by anything measured in this analysis.

The single largest and most consistent finding across every model, and the one most directly relevant to the project’s regional workforce mission, is that geography — where a student completes their postsecondary education — matters more than what credential they earn. Graduating within the region was the strongest predictor of sustained regional employment found among completers. But credential type is not irrelevant either: among college completers specifically, Associate-level and technical credentials showed a real, independent, additional association with staying regional, on top of the location effect.


The Story This Data Tells

Picture a graduating class in Northeast Minnesota. What happens to them over the next five, ten years, and why?

The pipeline starts to split immediately, along economic and ability- status lines. Economically disadvantaged students and students with disabilities are considerably less likely to complete any postsecondary credential at all — among the strongest demographic patterns in the entire analysis. But these same students, if they do complete a credential, don’t carry that disadvantage forward in the same form: much of what predicted non-completion in the first place turns out to run through how these students engaged with high school academically — CTE involvement, ACT participation, PSEO — rather than being a fixed consequence of the demographic category itself.

English learners face a genuinely different, more specific barrier — one that shows up at the front door, not throughout the building. English learners are meaningfully less likely to be sustainably attached to the regional workforce, and this disadvantage holds up fully no matter what else is accounted for — academic preparation, geography, whether they attempted college at all. But this barrier is entirely specific to students who don’t go on to complete a postsecondary credential. Among English learners who do complete a credential, there is no detectable disadvantage in staying regional, completing locally, or the level of credential earned. The English learner story in this data is a workforce- attachment story, not an academic-preparation story — and it resolves itself completely once a credential is in hand.

For those who do complete college, a second split happens — not about whether they succeed, but about where and how. Two students with identical demographics can take very different paths depending on how they engaged with high school. A student who took CTE courses and engaged with the ACT — regardless of score — is likely on a track toward a shorter, technical credential completed close to home, and likely to stay in the regional workforce afterward. A student who scored well on the ACT and pursued AP coursework or the SAT is likely headed toward a four-year degree, often completed outside the region — and less likely to return.

This is the pipeline’s central tension, visible in every model in this analysis: the very things that predict finishing a four-year degree — strong test scores, AP coursework, SAT participation — are the same things that predict leaving the region. Meanwhile, the pathways this project’s organization has less institutional visibility into promoting — CTE, technical credentials, PSEO — are consistently the ones associated with staying.

Geography reinforces this tension rather than resolving it. Students from more urban districts and Range school communities are considerably more likely to complete their postsecondary education within the region — this is the single largest set of effects found anywhere in this analysis — but this same geographic advantage barely registers when predicting whether a student completes college at all, or what type of credential they earn. Geography shapes where the funnel empties out, not how big the funnel is or what flows through it.

And underneath all of this, demographic disparities persist — but not always for the same reasons, and not always visible at the same point in the analysis. Some disparities — Special Education status, economic disadvantage, and, among completers, American Indian identity and Range school status — shrink substantially once a student’s academic engagement is accounted for, suggesting these groups may be channeled toward or away from certain pathways based on how school systems engage them, not fixed traits tied to the demographic category itself. Other disparities — particularly for Black, Hispanic, and Asian/Pacific Islander graduates — persist even after accounting for every other factor in this analysis, and in several cases only become visible once academic engagement is fully accounted for, suggesting they are not artifacts of differential program access but something this analysis cannot fully explain.

Put together, the data describes a region whose workforce retention depends less on whether people leave for stronger opportunities elsewhere and more on which door they walked through in high school — CTE and modest ACT engagement lead back to the region; AP coursework, strong ACT scores, and SAT participation lead away from it. This is not a story about talent leaving because the region has nothing to offer skilled workers — it’s a story about two genuinely different pipelines, one regionally anchored and one not, both running through the same high schools at the same time.
