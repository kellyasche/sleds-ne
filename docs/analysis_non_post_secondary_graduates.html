Employment outcomes distribution

The following tables provide the percentage of non-college graduates that were categorized in the various employment outcomes at 1-, 5-, and 10-years after graduating high school.

Key Findings
At Year 1, most non-graduates were concentrated in non-meaningful employment (35.0%) or had no Minnesota employment record (20.8%). Meaningful regional employment accounted for 19.2%, while meaningful employment elsewhere in Minnesota was minimal (5.6%). By Year 5, regional meaningful employment rose to 26.3% and became the largest single category, non-meaningful work declined to 27.3%, and meaningful employment outside the region more than doubled to 12.6%. By Year 10, regional meaningful employment held steady at 25.6% and meaningful employment outside the region grew slightly to 15.6%, but detachment from the state labor market surged to 36.7%.

Interpretation Over Time
Year 1 reflects a difficult transition for non-graduates, with most in unstable or disconnected positions. By Year 5, there is clear upward mobility as regional meaningful employment strengthens and non-meaningful work declines. This marks the high point of progress for those remaining in the workforce. However, by Year 10 the picture stabilizes, with little additional growth in meaningful employment. Instead, labor market detachment grows substantially, overtaking all other categories and becoming the dominant outcome.

Conclusion
Non-graduates make early gains toward meaningful regional employment, particularly within the first five years, but these gains plateau by Year 10. The growing share with no Minnesota employment record highlights long-term fragility in workforce attachment, suggesting that without intervention, a significant portion of this group will remain disconnected from the state’s labor market.

This analysis is going to focus on that 58% of the graduates, and explore relationships and patterns of the portion of this population that found meaningful employment in the region vs. other employment outcomes.


Methodology

The above tables show the importance of understanding how non-college grads are navigating their post-high school pathways with a significant portion of them being disconnected from the regional workforce. To better understand these pathways, we will explore the relationships between independent variables (Demographic, High school characteristics, and High school accomplishments) along with workforce outcomes at 1-, 5- and 10-years after graduating high school.

Since we provided the descriptive statistics above, we now need to analyze which factors relate most strongly to outcomes, and then see if we can use those factors to build a predictive model.

For exploring the relationships we will do the following;

  • For categorical predictors (e.g., Race/Ethnicity, Economic status, CTE achievement, PSEO participation), use cross-tabs with Cramer’s V and chi-square.
  • For continuous predictors (e.g., ACT, MCA scores, total CTE courses, county-level variables), use ANOVA to compare means across the employment states.
  • Run these analyses separately for grad.year.1, grad.year.5, grad.year.10 to see how relationships shift over time.

For building the predictive model, we will do the following;

  • Identify important predictors
    • Focus on effect sizes rather than just significance (since large N will make almost everything significant).
    • Highlight which variables consistently show moderate-to-strong associations with employment outcomes.
    • These become the candidates for inclusion in CART modeling.
  • For each year (1, 5, 10), run CART where the dependent variable is a binary comparison with “Meaningful emp region” as the reference:
    • Meaningful emp region vs. Meaningful emp MN
    • Meaningful emp region vs. Not meaningful, not attending ps
    • Meaningful emp region vs. No MN emp record, not attending ps

This will:

  • Show which factors distinguish students who stay employed meaningfully in their region from those in other outcomes.
  • Provide clear decision rules (e.g., “Non-college grads with high ACT scores and PSEO experience are far more likely to achieve meaningful regional employment.”).
  • Feed directly into actionable insights for policymakers.

Relationships

We will first explore the relationships between the independent variables and the 1-, 5- and 10-year outcomes.

Demographics

Key Findings
Among demographic variables, the strength of association with employment outcomes for non-college graduates ranges from weak to moderate. The relative influence is:

  1. Special Education Status – strongest predictor, with moderate effects (Cramer’s V ≈ 0.10–0.16), consistently linked to lower meaningful regional employment and higher disconnection.
  2. Economic Status – moderate influence (Cramer’s V ≈ 0.08–0.10), shaping job quality and access to meaningful regional employment, though less predictive of long-term detachment.
  3. Race/Ethnicity – weakest but still significant (Cramer’s V ≈ 0.05–0.06), highlighting disparities in meaningful regional employment and long-term workforce attachment.

Interpretation Over Time

  • Year 1: Disparities emerge immediately. Students of color (especially Black, Hispanic, and Asian/PI) and those with special education status face lower rates of meaningful regional employment and higher rates of detachment or non-meaningful work. Low-income students are more likely to be in non-meaningful jobs but show similar regional meaningful employment rates to their peers.
  • Year 5: Regional meaningful employment grows across groups, but gaps persist. Special education students remain more likely to be disconnected or in non-meaningful roles. Race/Ethnicity disparities widen as White and American Indian students gain stronger footholds in regional employment while Black, Hispanic, and Asian/PI students face higher disconnection. Economic status differences become more evident in job quality, with low-income students lagging.
  • Year 10: Patterns stabilize into long-term trajectories. Special education status remains the most consistent barrier to meaningful regional employment. Race/Ethnicity disparities deepen, with workforce detachment affecting more than half of Asian/PI and Hispanic students compared to about one-third of White students. Economic status continues to shape job quality, though disconnection rates converge across income levels.

Conclusion
Demographic characteristics meaningfully influence non-college graduates’ pathways into the workforce, with special education status standing out as the strongest and most consistent predictor of disadvantage. Race/Ethnicity disparities reveal systemic inequities, particularly in long-term workforce attachment for students of color, while economic status most strongly impacts job quality. Together, these findings show that while demographic effects are not the sole drivers of employment outcomes, they create enduring barriers that limit equitable access to meaningful regional employment.


RaceEthnicity

Key Findings
Across Years 1, 5, and 10, Race/Ethnicity consistently shows a statistically significant relationship with employment outcomes, though effect sizes remain weak (Cramer’s V = 0.047–0.063). White and American Indian students are consistently more likely to secure meaningful employment in the region (ranging from 17–27%), while Black, Hispanic, and Asian/PI students remain less represented in these jobs (typically 10–17%). Disconnection from the Minnesota labor market is persistently higher for Asian/PI and Hispanic students, climbing to more than half of each group by Year 10, compared with roughly one-third of White peers.

Interpretation Over Time

  • Year 1: Disparities emerge immediately, with White and American Indian students more likely to access meaningful regional jobs, while Black, Hispanic, and Asian/PI peers experience higher rates of non-meaningful employment or detachment.
  • Year 5: Meaningful regional employment rises across all groups, but gaps persist. Disconnection begins to sharply diverge for Asian/PI and Hispanic students, who are increasingly disconnected compared to White peers.
  • Year 10: Patterns solidify. White and American Indian students sustain stronger representation in meaningful regional employment, while long-term detachment dominates for Asian/PI and Hispanic students, with nearly half or more disconnected from the state’s workforce.

Conclusion
While the overall statistical association between Race/Ethnicity and employment outcomes is modest, the persistence of disparities is clear. White and American Indian non-graduates are better integrated into meaningful regional employment, while Black, Hispanic, and Asian/PI peers face enduring barriers and disproportionately higher rates of workforce disconnection. These results highlight systemic inequities in labor market access and underscore the need for targeted interventions to support underrepresented groups in achieving and sustaining meaningful regional employment.

Key Findings
At Year 1, Race/Ethnicity shows a statistically significant relationship with employment outcomes (χ²=188.6, p<.001), though the effect size is weak (Cramer’s V=0.047). White and American Indian students had the highest rates of meaningful regional employment (19.2% and 17.4%), while Black (11.7%), Hispanic (11.6%), and Asian/PI (10.0%) students were less likely to be employed meaningfully in the region. Non-meaningful employment was the most common outcome across all groups, with disconnection from Minnesota’s workforce particularly high among Asian/PI (35.0%) and Hispanic (32.5%) students.

Interpretation
The data suggest that in the first year after graduation, non-college graduates from underrepresented racial/ethnic groups are less likely to secure meaningful employment in the region compared to White or American Indian peers. Instead, they face higher rates of disconnection or concentration in non-meaningful employment. This points to early disparities in how different groups connect to the regional labor market, with meaningful regional employment disproportionately concentrated among White and American Indian students.

Conclusion
While the overall effect of Race/Ethnicity is weak, the disparities are clear: Black, Hispanic, and Asian/PI non-graduates are less likely to enter meaningful regional employment in the critical first year. These patterns highlight structural barriers that may hinder equitable access to quality regional jobs and suggest the need for targeted supports to improve early labor market attachment for underrepresented groups.

High school accomplishments

Key Findings
High School Accomplishments showed clear and consistent relationships with employment outcomes for non-college graduates, though the type and strength of predictors varied:

  1. ACT-related measures were among the strongest predictors:

    • ACT Taken (V=0.225 → 0.113 → 0.115) stood out as the single strongest categorical accomplishment variable, sharply dividing college-prep from workforce pathways.
    • ACT Composite Score (F=28.9 → 39.1 → 57.7) was the strongest continuous test score predictor, with higher scores tied to meaningful employment outside the region and lower scores tied to disconnection and non-meaningful work.

  2. PSEO Participation (V=0.135 → 0.052 → 0.081) mattered most in Year 1, increasing likelihood of postsecondary attendance but reducing early regional employment. Its predictive power weakened over time.

  3. CTE Achievement showed moderate effects (V≈0.06 across years): concentrators/completors were more likely to be in meaningful regional employment, while students with no CTE were more likely to be disconnected or in non-meaningful jobs. Total CTE Courses Taken (F=~6–20 range) reinforced this, with more courses linked to regional meaningful employment and fewer tied to detachment.

  4. MCA Scores (Math, Reading, Science) were consistent but modest predictors (F≈16–50 across years). Higher scores reduced risks of disconnection and non-meaningful work but also increased likelihood of leaving the region for meaningful employment. Lower scores anchored students locally but with weaker job quality and higher detachment risks.

Interpretation Over Time

  • Year 1: ACT and PSEO most strongly shaped pathways into postsecondary versus early workforce entry. CTE and MCA scores mainly separated students at risk of disconnection from those pursuing education or employment.
  • Year 5: ACT and MCA scores became stronger predictors of meaningful statewide employment. CTE continued to support regional employment, while PSEO’s influence weakened.
  • Year 10: ACT and MCA scores most sharply distinguished job quality and geography—higher scorers were more likely to leave the region for meaningful work, while lower scorers remained tied to the regional economy with greater vulnerability to non-meaningful jobs or disconnection. CTE maintained a stabilizing effect for regional attachment.

Conclusion
High School Accomplishments strongly predict both the quality and geography of employment outcomes. Academic measures (ACT, MCA) expanded opportunities but often steered students away from the region, while applied pathways (CTE) promoted regional attachment but did not eliminate risks of lower-quality jobs. Together, these findings highlight a tension: stronger academic preparation helps secure meaningful employment but often outside the region, while CTE fosters local workforce ties but with more mixed long-term outcomes.

Career and Technical Education (CTE) stands out: concentrators/completors and those who took more CTE courses were consistently more likely to secure meaningful regional jobs, with this advantage persisting even 10 years after graduation. Academic achievement (ACT, MCA) shaped pathways in the opposite direction: lower scorers more often transitioned earlier into regional employment, while higher scorers pursued postsecondary education and eventually secured meaningful work outside the region. PSEO participants followed a similar pattern—more likely to remain in school or move into statewide jobs, but less likely to anchor in regional employment.

Demographics highlighted persistent disparities. Students in special education were the least likely to secure meaningful regional jobs, instead concentrated in non-meaningful employment or disconnected from the workforce. Economic status also mattered: lower-income students were more likely to be region-bound but also more at risk of disconnection, while higher-income students more often pursued statewide opportunities. Race and ethnicity had small but notable effects, with white graduates slightly more likely to be in regional jobs. Gender was not analyzed for this region.

High school characteristics and local context provided important structural boundaries. Rural graduates were somewhat more likely to remain in meaningful regional work, while peers in more urban or mixed settings were more likely to move into statewide employment. County-level economic conditions were also influential: lower unemployment rates and higher relative wages boosted the chances of meaningful regional employment, while weaker counties increased risks of disconnection.

Taken together, the story is clear: for non-college graduates in Northeast Minnesota, CTE engagement is the most reliable pathway into meaningful regional employment. By contrast, stronger academic preparation and PSEO open broader opportunities but often outside the region. Demographics underscore where risks of disconnection are greatest, while county conditions reveal how local economies can either anchor graduates in regional jobs or push them away.

Post-pruned

The idea here is to allow the decision tree to grow fully and observe the CP value. There are two methods for identifying the CP code;

  1. Use the cp value with the lowest xerror from the base model.

  2. Use the cp value with the next lowest xerror that is within the standard error.

Overall Narrative

At 10 years after graduation, the pathway to sustaining meaningful regional employment depends most strongly on CTE engagement, moderated by SpecialEd needs, reading proficiency, and local economic conditions.

  • SpecialEd students, even with CTE backgrounds, remain at higher risk of long-term disconnection.
  • Local unemployment and wages shape whether academically weaker students can stay employed regionally, with low-income students sometimes more anchored regionally than higher-income peers in tough labor markets.

  • Year 1 (Early Career Launch):

Overall Narrative

Across all three comparisons, a clear pattern emerges:

  • Regional anchors: CTE engagement (especially completion), local wages, and in some cases low-income background.
  • Mobility markers: High ACT scores, ACT participation, and academic strength, which open pathways to statewide work.
  • Barriers to connection: SpecialEd needs, weak reading/math skills, and high local unemployment consistently predict disconnection from meaningful regional employment.

In short, regional employment is sustained when students engage deeply in CTE and are supported by favorable local labor markets, while academic high achievers are more mobile and SpecialEd students face enduring risks of disconnection.
